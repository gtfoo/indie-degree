# AIE-105 — Inference, Cost and Latency Engineering

**3 credits · 50 hours · 8 modules**

> The two questions a field engineer is actually asked in the room are what this will cost at our volume and how fast it will feel. Very few candidates can answer either with a number, and the ones who can are visibly different in the meeting. This course exists to make you one of them.

The questions a field engineer is actually asked in the room: what will this cost at our volume, and how fast will it feel. Very few candidates can answer either with a number.

**By the end you can**

- Model the token cost of a feature at a given volume, before building it
- Explain the prefill/decode split and what each implies for latency
- Apply caching, batching and streaming, and measure what each bought
- Read a serving stack — paged attention, continuous batching, quantisation — and reason about its trade-offs
- Degrade a model-backed feature gracefully rather than failing it

**Sitting rule.** Every item is completable in one sitting. Nothing exceeds 180 minutes, and anything over 90 declares its own checkpoints.

> **Advanced standing.** Advanced standing awarded 1 of 3 credits, for token economics: Career Side Quests already carries per-run token accounting and routes mechanical stages to the cheapest capable provider. That covers measuring cost after the fact. It does not cover modelling cost before building, which is the half that matters in a customer conversation and is not exemptable here. Serving, batching, caching and quantisation carried over nothing.

## Modules (43 items)

### M1 — The arithmetic of cost

*525 minutes*

- **M1.1 · Transformer Inference Arithmetic** — reading, 90 min, tier 0  
[Transformer Inference Arithmetic](https://kipp.ly/transformer-inference-arithmetic/)
  Read this first and read it properly. It is first-principles arithmetic with no experiments and no hard maths, and it is the thing that lets you answer a costing question in the room rather than promising to follow up.
- **M1.2 · Inference (CS336 Lecture 10)** — lecture, 90 min, tier 0  
[Stanford CS336 Language Modeling from Scratch I 2025](https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLoROMvodv4rOY23Y0BoGoBGgQ1zmU_MT_) — *Stanford Online*
  *Where:* Lecture 10, Inference.
  The inference lecture from the playlist. Prefill and decode, the KV cache, batching and speculative decoding, all derived rather than asserted.
- **M1.3 · Cost and inference chapters** — reading, 45 min, tier 0  
[AI Engineering](https://openlibrary.org/isbn/9781098166304)
  *Where:* The inference-optimisation chapter.
- **M1.4 · Instrument every call** — assignment, 60 min, tier 1 · **exemptable**
  *Already evidenced:* Career Side Quests already carries per-run token accounting across every stage, and routes mechanical stages to the cheapest capable provider. Cite the implementation and its cost readout rather than rebuilding it.
  Make every model call in one product report its input tokens, output tokens and cost, aggregated per user-visible operation rather than per API call.
- **M1.5 · Cost model before you build** — assignment, 120 min, tier 1
  This is the unexempted half. Pick a feature you have not built. Before writing any of it, produce a cost model: tokens per operation, operations per user per month, cost at 100, 10,000 and 1,000,000 users, and the single assumption the whole model is most sensitive to. Then build a thin version and compare the actual cost against your prediction. Report the error and where it came from.
  *Checkpoints:* model written and timestamped before implementation → thin version built → predicted versus actual reported with the error explained
- **M1.6 · The fine-tune decision** — assignment, 105 min, tier 2 · **optional**
  *Optional because:* AIE-107 M4.4 costs four routes over three years — frontier API, self-hosted open model, fine-tuned small model, and no model at all — and AIE-107 is studied first. This item costs three of those four and adds nothing AIE-107 does not already demand. Kept as an elective for the case where a specific capability gap is worth costing on its own; it is assessed if submitted, and completion does not wait for it.
  Should we fine-tune? is a top-three customer question and the honest answer is an arithmetic one. Take a real capability gap in one of your products and cost three routes to closing it: better prompting, retrieval, and fine-tuning. For each, state the engineering cost, the per-request cost at volume, the expected quality gain and how you would measure it, and what has to be true for it to be the right answer. You are not fine-tuning anything here — AIE-205 does that. You are learning to answer the question before the work, which is the part of it a field engineer actually owns.
  *Checkpoints:* gap defined → three routes costed → decision with its falsifying condition
- **M1.7 · Retention: token economics and cost modelling** — retention, 15 min, tier 1

### M2 — Prefill and decode

*405 minutes*

- **M2.1 · Efficiently Scaling Transformer Inference** — reading, 90 min, tier 0  
[Efficiently Scaling Transformer Inference](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.05102)
- **M2.2 · Making Deep Learning Go Brrrr From First Principles** — reading, 60 min, tier 0  
[Making Deep Learning Go Brrrr From First Principles](https://horace.io/brrr_intro.html)
  Compute, memory bandwidth, overhead. The three-way split that explains why an optimisation helped, or did nothing at all.
- **M2.3 · Measure the split** — assignment, 150 min, tier 1
  For one real feature, measure time to first token and inter-token latency separately across at least 100 requests, at three different input lengths. Show that prefill scales with input and decode does not, or explain why your data says otherwise. Report distributions — p50, p95, p99 — never a mean alone.
  *Checkpoints:* 100+ requests at three input lengths → TTFT and ITL separated → distributions reported
- **M2.4 · The latency budget** — assignment, 90 min, tier 2
  Write the end-to-end budget for one user-visible operation: every hop, its measured contribution, and which hop you would attack first. Include the parts that are not the model — network, cold start, your own code — because they are frequently the majority and are always the first thing a customer's engineer will ask about.
- **M2.5 · Retention: prefill, decode and latency structure** — retention, 15 min, tier 1

### M3 — Caching

*330 minutes*

- **M3.1 · Prompt caching** — reading, 45 min, tier 0  
[Prompt caching](https://docs.claude.com/en/docs/build-with-claude/prompt-caching)
- **M3.2 · litellm: routing, fallbacks and caching** — reading, 60 min, tier 0  
[BerriAI/litellm](https://github.com/BerriAI/litellm)
  *Where:* litellm/router.py — the routing, fallback and caching paths.
- **M3.3 · Cache what actually repeats** — assignment, 150 min, tier 1
  Find the repeated prefix in one of your products — a system prompt, a rubric, a document read on every request — and cache it.   *Checkpoints:* repeated prefix identified from real traffic → hit rate measured → cost and latency savings reported separately
- **M3.4 · When the cache is wrong** — assignment, 60 min, tier 2

### M4 — Batching and serving

*450 minutes*

- **M4.1 · PagedAttention and vLLM** — reading, 60 min, tier 0  
[Efficient Memory Management for Large Language Model Serving with PagedAttention](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.06180)
- **M4.2 · Sarathi-Serve: taming the throughput-latency tradeoff** — reading, 60 min, tier 0  
[Taming Throughput-Latency Tradeoff in LLM Inference with Sarathi-Serve](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.02310)

[vllm-project/vllm](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm)

- **M4.4 · Throughput versus latency, measured** — assignment, 180 min, tier 1
  Serve an open model yourself — rented GPU or llama.cpp locally — and produce the curve: requests per second against p95 latency, as concurrency rises. Find the knee. If you can only run this at small scale, say so explicitly and state what you expect to change at production scale and why.
  *Checkpoints:* model served locally or on rented hardware → concurrency swept → curve plotted with the knee identified
- **M4.5 · The curve a customer asks for** — assignment, 75 min, tier 2
  Convert that curve into the answer to 'we have 500 concurrent users, what do we need?' — hardware, expected p95, monthly cost, and the assumption most likely to make you wrong.
- **M4.6 · Retention: batching, scheduling and the serving stack** — retention, 15 min, tier 1

### M6 — Streaming and perceived latency

*330 minutes*

- **M6.1 · Fast Inference from Transformers via Speculative Decoding** — reading, 45 min, tier 0  
[Fast Inference from Transformers via Speculative Decoding](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.17192)
- **M6.2 · Medusa: multiple decoding heads** — reading, 45 min, tier 0  
[Medusa: Simple LLM Inference Acceleration Framework with Multiple Decoding Heads](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.10774)
- **M6.3 · Time to first token, on the text path** — assignment, 150 min, tier 1
  1 Percent More Fluent streams text to a person who is waiting. Measure time to first token as the user experiences it — from their action, not from your API call — then make one change and measure again. Report the distribution before and after, and the cost of the change. Scope this to the text path only: the audio path is measured in AIE-106 M4.6, and measuring them together hides which one you actually improved.
  *Checkpoints:* TTFT measured from user action → one change implemented → before and after distributions reported
- **M6.4 · Perceived versus measured** — assignment, 75 min, tier 2
  Identify one change that made the product feel faster without making it faster, and one that made it faster without feeling faster. Explain the mechanism in each case. This distinction is most of what a good demo is made of, and being able to name it is what separates an engineer from a salesperson in the room.

### M8 — Course project

*405 minutes*

- **M8.1 · Ship a measured improvement** — project, 165 min, tier 3
  Make one of your live products materially cheaper or faster, with the improvement measured on real traffic before and after. Materially means you would mention the number to a customer without embarrassment.
  *Checkpoints:* baseline measured on real traffic → change deployed → post-change measurement over comparable traffic
- **M8.2 · The economics report** — project, 105 min, tier 2
  Write the document you would hand a customer evaluating this feature: what it costs per operation, what it costs at their volume, the p50 and p95 latency, what you tried that did not help, and the volume at which the architecture would have to change. Add the commercial frame a buyer actually uses: cost per interaction set against the value of that interaction, and the gross margin the feature leaves at their volume. A cost with no value beside it cannot be acted on.
  *Checkpoints:* cost per operation and at volume → latency distributions → failed optimisations included → cost per interaction set against value and margin
- **M8.3 · Publish** — project, 75 min, tier 3
  Publish this as the next chapter of your public engineering log — one growing site, not a sixth standalone post. The chapter must stand alone on its own page for a reader arriving cold, and must connect backwards to at least one result from an earlier chapter. Publish at a stable public URL with the measurement harness reachable, so a reader can rerun the benchmark against their own setup.
- **M8.4 · Oral defense** — defense, 60 min, tier 4
  Three unscripted questions generated from your report at defense time, answered aloud without notes.
  *Question shapes:*
    - A prospect has 50,000 daily active users. From your numbers, what do they spend a month and what do they feel?
    - Your p95 is three times your p50. Explain that to a customer who has just noticed it.
    - Which of your measurements would you least want a performance engineer to audit?
