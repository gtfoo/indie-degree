# AIE-201 — Mathematics for Machine Learning

**3 credits · 70 hours · 5 modules**

> Targeted, not an undergraduate sequence. The failure mode of maths-for-ML is spending a year on proofs and never arriving at the machine learning, so every module here ends in something computed by hand on an object from your own work and then checked against a library. You are not being taught to prove theorems. You are being taught to read a paper's equations without skipping them, and to know what an optimiser is actually doing when a training run goes wrong.

Targeted, not an undergraduate sequence. Only what is needed to read the papers and understand what training is doing.

**By the end you can**

- Manipulate matrices, eigendecompositions and projections fluently enough to read architecture papers
- Differentiate through a computational graph by hand
- Reason about distributions, expectation and variance in a loss function
- Explain gradient descent and its common variants from the mathematics, not the metaphor

**Sitting rule.** Every item is completable in one sitting. Nothing exceeds 180 minutes, and anything over 90 declares its own checkpoints.

### M1 — Linear algebra you actually need

*900 minutes*

- **M1.1 · Essence of linear algebra, the geometry** — lecture, 150 min, tier 0  
[Essence of linear algebra](https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLZHQObOWTQDPD3MizzM2xVFitgF8hE_ab) — *3Blue1Brown*
  *Where:* Chapters 1–8: vectors, span, linear transformations as matrices, matrix multiplication as composition, and determinants.
- **M1.2 · Essence of linear algebra, change of basis and eigenvectors** — lecture, 150 min, tier 0  
[Essence of linear algebra](https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLZHQObOWTQDPD3MizzM2xVFitgF8hE_ab) — *3Blue1Brown*
  *Where:* Chapters 9–15: dot products, change of basis, eigenvectors and eigenvalues, abstract vector spaces.
- **M1.3 · Linear algebra, written down** — reading, 120 min, tier 0  
[Mathematics for Machine Learning](https://mml-book.github.io/)
  *Where:* Chapter 2, Linear Algebra, through 2.7 on linear mappings. The notation, after the geometry — this order round, because the pictures make the symbols readable and not the reverse.
- **M1.4 · Matrix decompositions** — reading, 120 min, tier 0  
[Mathematics for Machine Learning](https://mml-book.github.io/)
  *Where:* Chapter 4, Matrix Decompositions — eigendecomposition, the singular value decomposition, and 4.6 on low-rank approximation.
- **M1.5 · Decompose your own corpus** — assignment, 180 min, tier 1
  Build a term-document matrix from Indie Degree's own 203 verified resources — titles and notes, no embeddings and no API calls. Compute its SVD with numpy. Report the singular value spectrum, choose an effective rank with a threshold you state before looking, and reconstruct the matrix at that rank. Then do the part that matters: open the top three right singular vectors, read which resources load on each, and say in a sentence what topic each one found. If a component is not interpretable, say that instead of inventing a theme.
  *Checkpoints:* Term-document matrix built and its shape reported → SVD computed and singular values plotted → Effective rank chosen with a stated threshold → Interpretation written against the actual documents
- **M1.6 · Eigenvectors by hand, then by library** — assignment, 165 min, tier 1
  Take one 2×2 and one 3×3 matrix. Find the eigenvalues by writing out and solving the characteristic polynomial, then find and normalise the eigenvectors — on paper, showing the steps. Verify against numpy.linalg.eig. Where the signs or the ordering differ from yours, explain why that is not a disagreement. Then answer in two sentences: what does it mean that a covariance matrix is symmetric, and what does that guarantee about its eigenvectors.
  *Checkpoints:* Characteristic polynomial written out for the 3×3 → Eigenvalues solved by hand → Eigenvectors solved and normalised → numpy comparison run, and any disagreement explained
- **M1.7 · Retention: decompositions and what they are for** — retention, 15 min, tier 1

### M2 — Differentiating a computational graph

*840 minutes*

- **M2.1 · Essence of calculus, derivatives and the chain rule** — lecture, 120 min, tier 0  
[Essence of calculus](https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLZHQObOWTQDMsr9K-rj53DwVRMYO3t5Yr) — *3Blue1Brown*
  *Where:* Chapters 1–6: the derivative as a limit, the power and product rules, and the chain rule.
- **M2.2 · Essence of calculus, exponentials and approximation** — lecture, 120 min, tier 0  
[Essence of calculus](https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLZHQObOWTQDMsr9K-rj53DwVRMYO3t5Yr) — *3Blue1Brown*
  *Where:* Chapters 7–12: implicit differentiation, limits, exponentials, and Taylor series.
- **M2.3 · Vector calculus and backpropagation** — reading, 120 min, tier 0  
[Mathematics for Machine Learning](https://mml-book.github.io/)
  *Where:* Chapter 5, Vector Calculus — particularly 5.4 on gradients of matrices and 5.6, which derives backpropagation as repeated application of the chain rule.
- **M2.4 · Backpropagation by hand** — assignment, 180 min, tier 1
  Take a two-layer network with three inputs, two hidden units, a sigmoid, and a squared-error loss. Choose concrete weights. Compute the forward pass by hand. Then derive every partial derivative of the loss with respect to every weight symbolically, and evaluate them numerically. Check against PyTorch autograd. Report agreement to a stated number of decimal places, and if anything disagrees, find the sign error rather than adjusting the tolerance.
  *Checkpoints:* Forward pass computed by hand with numbers written down → Every partial derivative derived symbolically → Numeric gradients produced for one input → Autograd comparison run and agreement reported to a stated precision
- **M2.5 · Differentiate a loss you actually use** — assignment, 150 min, tier 1
  Find a loss function in something you have shipped or in a library you depend on, and write it down formally from the source rather than from memory. Derive its gradient with respect to the model's logits. For cross-entropy over a softmax the answer collapses to something famously simple — if you get that, show the cancellation step by step rather than quoting the result.
  *Checkpoints:* The loss written formally from the code that computes it → Gradient derived with respect to the logits → Result checked numerically
- **M2.6 · Where the chain rule explains the bug** — assignment, 135 min, tier 2
  Build a deliberately deep stack of sigmoid layers and measure the gradient magnitude at each layer. Show the decay numerically rather than asserting it. Then explain, from the derivative of the sigmoid itself, why the decay is multiplicative and roughly what factor per layer you should expect. Change one thing — the activation, the initialisation, or a residual connection — and measure again. The claim to defend is which of those you changed and why it was the right one.
  *Checkpoints:* A deep chain constructed and its gradient magnitudes measured per layer → The saturation point identified numerically → A fix applied and re-measured
- **M2.7 · Retention: the chain rule through a graph** — retention, 15 min, tier 1

### M3 — Probability, and what a loss function is measuring

*840 minutes*

- **M3.1 · Probability and distributions** — reading, 180 min, tier 0  
[Mathematics for Machine Learning](https://mml-book.github.io/)
  *Where:* Chapter 6, Probability and Distributions — through 6.5 on Gaussians, and 6.6 on conjugacy and the exponential family.
- **M3.2 · Where the model meets the data** — reading, 150 min, tier 0  
[Mathematics for Machine Learning](https://mml-book.github.io/)
  *Where:* Chapter 8, When Models Meet Data — 8.1 to 8.3: empirical risk minimisation, parameter estimation, and what maximum likelihood is actually assuming.
- **M3.3 · The probabilistic view of a model** — lecture, 150 min, tier 0  
[Stanford CS229: Machine Learning Course \| Summer 2019 (Anand Avati)](https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLoROMvodv4rNH7qL6-efu_q2_bPuy0adh) — *Stanford Online*
  *Where:* The lectures on the probabilistic interpretation of least squares and on generalised linear models — where a loss stops being a choice and becomes a consequence of a distributional assumption.
- **M3.4 · Cross-entropy is not a choice** — assignment, 180 min, tier 1
  Derive cross-entropy from maximum likelihood for a categorical model, showing every step. Then do the same for a Gaussian with fixed variance and arrive at squared error. Write the conclusion in your own words: what distributional assumption are you making every time you reach for MSE, and name one situation in your own work where that assumption is wrong.
  *Checkpoints:* The likelihood written for a categorical model → Log taken and negated, with each step justified → The result matched against a library implementation → The Gaussian case derived to squared error
- **M3.5 · The variance in your own numbers** — assignment, 165 min, tier 2
  Take a set of scores you have actually measured — an eval run, a latency sample, anything with more than thirty observations. Compute a confidence interval, by bootstrap or analytically, and say which you chose and why. Then find a comparison you or someone else has previously stated as a fact — 'X is better than Y' — and check whether the interval supports it. The valuable outcome here is discovering that a difference you believed is inside the noise.
  *Checkpoints:* A real measured set of scores assembled → Bootstrap or analytic interval computed → A previously stated comparison re-examined against the interval
- **M3.6 · Retention: likelihood, loss, and what follows from what** — retention, 15 min, tier 1

### M4 — Optimisation

*840 minutes*

- **M4.1 · Continuous optimisation** — reading, 150 min, tier 0  
[Mathematics for Machine Learning](https://mml-book.github.io/)
  *Where:* Chapter 7, Continuous Optimization — gradient descent with momentum, stochastic gradient descent, constrained optimisation and duality.
- **M4.2 · Optimisation as it actually behaves in deep networks** — reading, 150 min, tier 0  
[Deep Learning](https://www.deeplearningbook.org/)
  *Where:* Chapter 8, Optimization for Training Deep Models — especially 8.2 on the challenges, and 8.5 on adaptive learning rates.
- **M4.3 · Three optimisers from their update rules** — assignment, 180 min, tier 1
  Implement gradient descent, gradient descent with momentum, and Adam in numpy — from the update rules as written in the reading, not from a library and not from memory. Run all three on the same two-dimensional objective with a known minimum, and plot the trajectories. Include Adam's bias-correction terms and then run it once with them removed, so you can show what they actually do in the first few steps.
  *Checkpoints:* Plain gradient descent implemented and converging → Momentum added, with the update rule written above the code → Adam implemented including bias correction → All three plotted on the same objective
- **M4.4 · Why Adam, and when it is worse** — assignment, 165 min, tier 2
  Construct one objective where Adam clearly beats SGD and one where it clearly does not, and measure both. Then explain the difference from the update rules themselves — what the per-parameter scaling buys you and what it costs. The second case is the point of the assignment: 'use Adam' is the default advice, and a submission that cannot produce a case against it has not understood why it works.
  *Checkpoints:* A case constructed where Adam wins, and measured → A case constructed where plain SGD wins, and measured → The mechanism explained from the update rules
- **M4.5 · Make training fail, three ways** — assignment, 180 min, tier 1
  Deliberately break the same training run three ways: a learning rate that diverges, an initialisation that kills the gradient, and inputs on wildly different scales. Record the loss curve and the gradient norms for each. Then write the artifact that makes this worth doing — a short table mapping the symptom you would actually observe to the cause, so that next time you see it in real training you recognise it rather than guessing.
  *Checkpoints:* Failure by learning rate produced and its signature recorded → Failure by initialisation produced and recorded → Failure by conditioning or scaling produced and recorded → A short diagnostic table written from the three signatures
- **M4.6 · Retention: optimiser behaviour and failure signatures** — retention, 15 min, tier 1

### M5 — The mathematics of something you already use

*780 minutes*

- **M5.1 · What a network is computing** — lecture, 75 min, tier 0  
[Neural networks](https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLZHQObOWTQDNU6R1_67000Dx_ZCJB-3pi) — *3Blue1Brown*
  *Where:* Chapters 1–2: a layer as a matrix multiplication followed by a nonlinearity, and what the parameters mean.
- **M5.2 · Scaled dot-product attention, the equation only** — reading, 120 min, tier 0  
[Attention Is All You Need](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762)
  *Where:* Section 3.2.1 and its footnote on why the dot products are scaled. Read this for the mathematics alone; the architecture is AIE-204's job.
- **M5.3 · Why the square root of d_k** — assignment, 150 min, tier 2
  The paper justifies dividing by the square root of the key dimension in a single footnote. Derive it: show that the dot product of two independent vectors with unit-variance components has variance equal to the dimension. Then demonstrate numerically what a large-variance input does to softmax, and measure what that does to the gradient. This is the assignment where the whole course pays off — one footnote in a paper you have used for two years, turned into something you can derive.
  *Checkpoints:* Variance of a dot product of independent unit-variance vectors derived → The effect on softmax demonstrated numerically at two dimensionalities → The gradient consequence measured
- **M5.4 · The mathematics of one system you built: derivation** — project, 180 min, tier 2
  Pick one component of something you have shipped and write down what it is doing mathematically — the similarity metric in a retrieval path, the scoring function in a pipeline, the aggregation in a ranker. Derive its behaviour rather than describing it, and check each claim against the system as it actually runs. State at least one thing the derivation tells you that you did not know before writing it, or say plainly that it told you nothing new.
  *Checkpoints:* System chosen and the mathematical claim stated → The derivation written out → Every claim checked numerically against the running system
- **M5.5 · The mathematics of one system you built: publish** — project, 180 min, tier 3
  Publish the derivation as a chapter of the public engineering log. It must stand alone on its own page and must connect backwards to at least one earlier chapter's result. Notation is the thing readers give up on: define it once, use it consistently, and do not switch between index and matrix form without saying so.
  *Checkpoints:* Written for a reader who has not seen the system → Notation defined once and used consistently → Published, and linked from the public log → Connected backwards to an earlier chapter's result
- **M5.6 · Oral defense** — defense, 60 min, tier 4
  Three unscripted questions generated from your own submissions at defense time, answered aloud without notes and without a calculator. Mathematics is where notes hide the gap most effectively, which is why there are none.
  *Question shapes:*
    - You divided by the square root of d_k. Talk me through the variance argument without writing anything down.
    - Your gradient check agreed to six decimal places. What would it have looked like if you had a sign error in exactly one weight, and would you have noticed?
    - You said the difference in your eval numbers was inside the noise. Someone disagrees and says thirty samples is plenty. Answer them.
- **M5.7 · Retention: the whole course, cold** — retention, 15 min, tier 1

## Completion

- All eleven assignments, both project parts and the defense submitted. Readings and lectures are tier 0 and are tracked but never counted toward mastery.
- Deterministic. Each item's tier sets its weight; panel scores enter as per-criterion values; nothing is rounded up. Computed in TypeScript, never by a model.
- Sets initial mastery on all four mathematics skills. FSRS then decays them, and this is the course where that matters most — a derivation you cannot reproduce six months later was never knowledge, it was a transcription.
