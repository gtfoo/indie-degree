## Modules (37 items)

### M1 — Tokenisation, which is where the bill comes from

*720 minutes*

- **M1.1 · Building a byte-pair encoder** — lecture, 180 min, tier 0  
[Neural Networks: Zero to Hero](https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLAqhIrjkxbuWI23v9cThsA9GvCAUhRvKZ)
  *Where:* The tokenizer video — building BPE from scratch, and the section on why so many model failures are really tokeniser failures.
- **M1.2 · The three tokenisation algorithms in use** — reading, 165 min, tier 0  
[Introduction - Hugging Face NLP Course](https://huggingface.co/learn/nlp-course/chapter1/1)
  *Where:* The tokenizers chapters — byte-pair encoding, WordPiece and Unigram, and the section on training a new tokeniser from an old one.
- **M1.3 · Byte-pair encoding from scratch** — assignment, 180 min, tier 1
  Implement byte-pair encoding: the merge-training loop, the encoder and the decoder. Train it on a corpus of your own. Verify that encode-then-decode is the identity on held-out text including punctuation, emoji and whitespace runs — that is where naive implementations quietly lose bytes. Then vary the vocabulary size and measure compression, so the trade between vocabulary and sequence length is a curve you have seen rather than a claim you have read.
  *Checkpoints:* Merge training loop implemented over a real corpus → Encode and decode round-trip verified on held-out text → Vocabulary size varied and compression measured → Compared against a reference tokeniser on the same text
- **M1.4 · What your tokeniser costs in every language** — assignment, 180 min, tier 2
  Take the same passage in every language 1 Percent More Fluent supports and measure how many tokens each becomes under the tokeniser you actually pay for. Convert that into money at the real per-token price and into context-window consumption. The ratio between the cheapest and the most expensive language will be larger than you expect, and it is a fact about your product's economics, not a curiosity. Finish by naming one thing you could do about it and what it would cost.
  *Checkpoints:* The same content prepared in several languages → Token counts measured per language with a production tokeniser → Cost and context consequences computed at a real price → A mitigation considered and its trade stated
- **M1.5 · Retention: tokenisation and its consequences** — retention, 15 min, tier 1

### M2 — Attention, implemented

*840 minutes*

- **M2.1 · The illustrated transformer** — reading, 105 min, tier 0  
[The Illustrated Transformer](https://jalammar.github.io/illustrated-transformer/)
- **M2.2 · Attention Is All You Need, in full** — reading, 120 min, tier 0  
[Attention Is All You Need](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762)
  *Where:* Sections 3 and 4 entirely. AIE-201 read only 3.2.1, for the scaling argument; this is the rest of the architecture, including why multi-head exists at all.
- **M2.3 · Let's build GPT: the attention half** — lecture, 180 min, tier 0  
[Let's build GPT: from scratch, in code, spelled out](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kCc8FmEb1nY)
  *Where:* Through the self-attention sections — the mathematical trick with the triangular mask, then single and multi-head attention.
- **M2.4 · Multi-head self-attention, from scratch** — assignment, 180 min, tier 1
  Implement scaled dot-product attention, then causal masking, then multi-head — without opening a reference. Verify the mask does what you think by feeding a sequence where a leak would change the output at position zero. Then load identical weights into torch.nn.MultiheadAttention and confirm your output matches to a stated tolerance. The weight-loading step is the assignment: matching on random weights proves nothing, and getting the layout right is where the understanding is.
  *Checkpoints:* Single-head attention implemented and shape-checked → Causal mask applied and verified to block future positions → Extended to multi-head with heads genuinely independent → Output matched against torch.nn.MultiheadAttention on identical weights
- **M2.5 · What the heads are actually doing** — assignment, 150 min, tier 2
  Extract and visualise attention weights from a small trained model across heads and layers. Find one head whose behaviour you can describe defensibly — attending to the previous token, to a matching delimiter, to the subject of the sentence — and evidence it across several inputs rather than one. Then find a head you cannot characterise and say so. The second half is the assignment: attention maps are famously easy to over-read, and a submission where every head has a tidy story is a submission that invented some.
  *Checkpoints:* Attention weights extracted per head per layer → Visualised over real input → One head given a defensible description → One head reported as not interpretable
- **M2.6 · Self-attention in the NLP lineage** — lecture, 90 min, tier 0  
[Stanford CS224N: Natural Language Processing with Deep Learning](https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLoROMvodv4rOSH4v6133s9LFPRHjEmbmJ)
  *Where:* The self-attention and transformers lectures — attention as it arrived from sequence-to-sequence models, which is the context the paper assumes.
- **M2.7 · Retention: attention mechanics** — retention, 15 min, tier 1

### M3 — A GPT you can explain line by line

*900 minutes*

- **M3.1 · Let's build GPT: assembling the model** — lecture, 180 min, tier 0  
[Let's build GPT: from scratch, in code, spelled out](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kCc8FmEb1nY)
  *Where:* The remainder — blocks, residual connections, layer normalisation placement, and the training loop.
- **M3.2 · The annotated transformer** — reading, 165 min, tier 0  
[The Annotated Transformer](https://nlp.seas.harvard.edu/annotated-transformer/)
- **M3.3 · Build the model** — assignment, 180 min, tier 1
  Assemble the full model from your attention implementation: embeddings, stacked blocks with residuals and normalisation, and a language-modelling head. Compute the parameter count by hand from the dimensions before asking the framework, and reconcile any difference. Train it on a small corpus until it produces text that is recognisably from that distribution. It will be bad text; that is fine and expected at this scale.
  *Checkpoints:* Token and position embeddings, blocks, and output head assembled → Parameter count computed by hand and matched → Trained on a small corpus with the loss falling → Text generated that is recognisably from the training distribution
- **M3.4 · Sampling is a design decision** — assignment, 180 min, tier 2
  Implement greedy decoding, temperature scaling, top-k and nucleus sampling on your own model. Run them on identical prompts with identical seeds and measure — repetition rate and distinct-n, not just how the output reads. Then connect it back: your shipped products set these parameters, probably to whatever the default was. Say what you would now set them to and why. This is the item where a from-scratch exercise pays a dividend in production.
  *Checkpoints:* Greedy, temperature, top-k and nucleus sampling implemented → Each run on identical prompts and seeds → A quantitative measure of diversity and repetition reported → A recommendation made for one of your own products
- **M3.5 · Building an LLM, the book-length treatment** — reading, 180 min, tier 0  
[Build a Large Language Model (From Scratch)](https://openlibrary.org/isbn/9781633437166)
  *Where:* The chapters on coding the attention mechanism and on implementing a GPT model from scratch — read after your own implementation exists, as a check on it rather than as instructions.
- **M3.6 · Retention: the decoder stack, component by component** — retention, 15 min, tier 1

### M4 — What changed since 2017

*780 minutes*

- **M4.1 · Decoder-only architectures and their variants** — lecture, 150 min, tier 0  
[Stanford CS25 - Transformers United](https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLoROMvodv4rNiJRchCzutFw5ItR_Z27CM)
  *Where:* The lectures covering decoder-only models and the architectural changes that separate a modern model from the 2017 paper.
- **M4.2 · FlashAttention** — reading, 105 min, tier 0  
[FlashAttention: Fast and Memory-Efficient Exact Attention with IO-Awareness](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.14135)
- **M4.3 · Three modern components, measured one at a time** — assignment, 180 min, tier 2
  Take your working model as the 2017 baseline and substitute three modern components one at a time: RMSNorm for LayerNorm, a gated feed-forward such as SwiGLU for the standard one, and rotary position embeddings for learned ones. Measure each independently, holding parameter count roughly constant. Report which earned its place at your scale and which did not. Modern models use all three; at a few million parameters on a laptop you may find one of them does nothing, and reporting that honestly is worth more than reproducing the expected ordering.
  *Checkpoints:* Baseline model fixed and its result recorded → RMSNorm substituted and measured → A gated feed-forward substituted and measured → Rotary position embeddings substituted and measured → Parameter counts reconciled across variants
- **M4.4 · The engineering decisions in a modern stack** — lecture, 165 min, tier 0  
[Stanford CS336 Language Modeling from Scratch I 2025](https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLoROMvodv4rOY23Y0BoGoBGgQ1zmU_MT_)
  *Where:* The lectures on architecture variants and on the systems decisions behind training a language model — attribution note: cite the course playlist, since the individually reuploaded copies are not authorised.
- **M4.5 · Mixture of experts** — reading, 90 min, tier 0  
[Switch Transformers: Scaling to Trillion Parameter Models with Simple and Efficient Sparsity](https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.03961)
- **M4.6 · A KV cache, and what it buys** — assignment, 75 min, tier 1
  Add a key-value cache to your model's generation path. First prove it is correct: cached and uncached generation must produce identical tokens for the same seed, and if they do not, the bug is subtle and worth finding. Then measure time per generated token with and without, across several sequence lengths, and state the complexity you have changed and the memory you have traded for it.
  *Checkpoints:* Cache implemented for incremental decoding → Output verified identical to the uncached path → Time per token measured with and without, across sequence lengths
- **M4.7 · Retention: modern architectural components** — retention, 15 min, tier 1

### M5 — Reproduce a published result

*840 minutes*

- **M5.1 · Reproducing GPT-2** — lecture, 180 min, tier 0  
[Let's reproduce GPT-2 (124M)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=l8pRSuU81PU)
- **M5.2 · nanoGPT as the reference** — reading, 165 min, tier 0  
[karpathy/nanoGPT](https://github.com/karpathy/nanoGPT)
  *Where:* model.py and train.py — read only after your own implementation exists, and read for the decisions rather than the syntax.
- **M5.3 · The reproduction: run it** — project, 180 min, tier 2
  Pick a published result you can attempt at a scale you can afford — a validation loss on a named dataset at a named parameter count. State the target and your scale before starting. Run it. Record what you got, what it cost in compute and wall-clock, and every deviation from the published setup you had to make. Failing to hit the number is an expected outcome; not knowing why is the failure.
  *Checkpoints:* A published number chosen as the target, with its source cited → The scale you can actually afford stated in advance → The run completed and its result recorded → Compute and wall-clock cost accounted for
- **M5.4 · The reproduction: account for the gap** — project, 150 min, tier 2
  Enumerate every way your run differed from the published one — data, tokeniser, sequence length, batch size, schedule, precision, training tokens. For each, estimate the direction and rough size of its effect. Add them up and compare against the gap you actually observed. What is left over is the part you cannot explain, and stating it plainly is the point of the exercise. A gap analysis that exactly accounts for the difference is almost always a gap analysis that was fitted to it.
  *Checkpoints:* Every deviation from the published setup enumerated → Each one's likely direction and rough magnitude estimated → The estimates summed and compared against the observed gap → The residual stated as unexplained
- **M5.5 · minGPT, for what it leaves out** — reading, 150 min, tier 0  
[karpathy/minGPT](https://github.com/karpathy/minGPT)
  *Where:* model.py — the minimal version, read specifically for what it omits relative to nanoGPT and why that is acceptable in a teaching implementation.
- **M5.6 · Retention: reproduction and its failure modes** — retention, 15 min, tier 1

### M6 — Publish, and defend

*720 minutes*

- **M6.1 · The annotated implementation, published** — project, 180 min, tier 3
  Publish your implementation as a public repository. It must run end to end from a clean checkout on a laptop, and its README must say plainly what it is for and what it is not — it is not a better nanoGPT and should not claim to be. The value is the annotation: every non-obvious line explains why it is there, which is the artifact that distinguishes having built one from having copied one.
  *Checkpoints:* Repository public, with a README that states what it is and is not → Every non-obvious line carrying a comment that says why → Runnable end to end from a clean checkout → A stated relationship to nanoGPT and minGPT
- **M6.2 · Every choice, and what it buys** — project, 180 min, tier 3
  Write the chapter that goes with the repository: every architectural decision, its alternative, and what choosing it bought. Where you measured, cite the measurement. Where you inherited a choice because everyone does it, say so and mark it as unjustified rather than dressing it up. That distinction — measured versus inherited — is the most useful thing this document can contain, and almost no published write-up makes it.
  *Checkpoints:* Each architectural decision listed with its alternative → Measurements cited where they exist → Choices with no measurement marked as inherited rather than justified → Published and connected backwards to an earlier chapter
- **M6.3 · Transformer variants and efficiency, a second lineage** — lecture, 165 min, tier 0  
[CMU Advanced NLP Fall 2024](https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PL8PYTP1V4I8D4BeyjwWczukWq9d8PNyZp) — *Graham Neubig*
  *Where:* The lectures on transformer variants and on efficiency — a different teaching tradition from the Stanford lineage, useful for the places the two disagree.
- **M6.4 · Read a new architecture cold** — assignment, 120 min, tier 2
  Take a model card or technical report published after this course was registered, and read its architecture section cold. Enumerate what differs from what you built. Then pick the one that matters most and explain what it is mechanically doing and what problem it solves — not what the report says it does, but what you can work out from your own implementation. This is the outcome the whole course exists to produce, and it is the only item that uses material nobody could have prepared you for.
  *Checkpoints:* A model released after this course was written, chosen → Its architecture section read and summarised → Differences from your implementation enumerated → One difference explained mechanically, not just named
- **M6.5 · Oral defense** — defense, 60 min, tier 4
  Three unscripted questions generated from your own implementation and write-up at defense time, answered aloud without notes and without the code open. This is the defense that carries the transformers capability claim, so it is the one that matters most on a CV.
  *Question shapes:*
    - Open your model file mentally and walk me from a token id to a logit. Where does the residual stream get written to, and by what?
    - You said RoPE made no measurable difference at your scale. At what scale would you expect it to, and why that scale?
    - Your reproduction missed the published number by a margin you partly explained. A reviewer says the unexplained residual means your implementation is wrong. Answer them.
- **M6.6 · Retention: the whole architecture, cold** — retention, 15 min, tier 1
