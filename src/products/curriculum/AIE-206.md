# AIE-206 — Reinforcement Learning

**3 credits · 65 hours · 5 modules**

> The lowest immediate employability of the deep track, and placed late on purpose rather than apologised for. Its value is that RLHF and reasoning-model training are built on it, and AIE-205 taught preference optimisation as a method without ever explaining the problem it was avoiding. This course explains the problem. By the end, DPO's whole appeal — that it removes the reinforcement learning — is something you can argue about rather than repeat.

Lowest immediate employability of the deep track, and the necessary grounding for RLHF and for reasoning-model training. Placed late on purpose.

**By the end you can**

- Formalise a problem as an MDP
- Implement a value-based and a policy-gradient agent
- Explain the credit assignment problem and how RLHF inherits it
- Connect policy optimisation to preference optimisation from AIE-205

## Modules (30 items)

### M1 — Markov decision processes

*780 minutes*

- **M1.1 · The reinforcement learning problem** — reading, 180 min, tier 0  
[Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction](https://openlibrary.org/isbn/9780262039246)
  *Where:* Chapters 1 and 3 — the problem setting, then finite Markov decision processes, returns, and the value functions.
- **M1.2 · The framework, with code** — reading, 150 min, tier 0  
[Welcome to the Deep Reinforcement Learning Course](https://huggingface.co/learn/deep-rl-course/unit0/introduction)
  *Where:* Unit 1 — the agent-environment loop and the reward hypothesis — and the introduction to the Bellman equation in unit 2.
- **M1.3 · Formalise something of your own as an MDP** — assignment, 180 min, tier 1
  Take a real decision problem from one of your own products — which model to route a request to, when to re-index, how many retrieval results to fetch — and formalise it as an MDP. Define the state, action and reward precisely enough that someone else could implement it. Choose a discount factor and justify it in terms of the actual decision horizon rather than by using 0.99 because everyone does. Then name one place where the Markov property does not hold in reality, because that is almost always true and pretending otherwise is where applied RL goes wrong.
  *Checkpoints:* A real decision problem chosen from your own systems → States, actions, transitions and rewards defined precisely → The discount factor chosen with a stated justification → One property of the formalisation shown to be false in reality
- **M1.4 · Where the framing breaks** — assignment, 150 min, tier 2
  Take the MDP you just wrote and attack it. Where is the state only partially observable? Where does the reward arrive so late that credit assignment becomes hopeless? Where would exploration be unacceptable because it means giving real users bad answers? Attempt a reformulation. Then record the judgement this course wants you to be able to make: given all that, is reinforcement learning the right tool for this problem, and if not, what is.
  *Checkpoints:* Partial observability identified concretely in the chosen problem → The consequence for a learned policy stated → A reformulation attempted → A judgement recorded on whether RL is the right tool at all
- **M1.5 · Dynamic programming, before learning** — reading, 105 min, tier 0  
[Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction](https://openlibrary.org/isbn/9780262039246)
  *Where:* Chapter 4 — policy evaluation, policy improvement and policy iteration, with the model known. Everything later is this without the model.
- **M1.6 · Retention: MDPs and their assumptions** — retention, 15 min, tier 1

### M2 — Value-based methods

*780 minutes*

- **M2.1 · Monte Carlo and temporal difference** — reading, 165 min, tier 0  
[Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction](https://openlibrary.org/isbn/9780262039246)
  *Where:* Chapters 5 and 6 — Monte Carlo prediction and control, then TD learning, SARSA and Q-learning.
- **M2.2 · Q-learning and deep Q-networks** — lecture, 150 min, tier 0  
[Welcome to the Deep Reinforcement Learning Course](https://huggingface.co/learn/deep-rl-course/unit0/introduction)
  *Where:* Units 2 and 3 — tabular Q-learning, then the deep Q-network and the tricks that make it work.
- **M2.3 · Tabular Q-learning from the update rule** — assignment, 180 min, tier 1
  Implement tabular Q-learning on a small environment from the update rule as written in the book, not from an example. Get it to converge. Then vary the exploration schedule and show what happens with too little exploration — the agent finds a mediocre policy and stops, confidently. Finally, print the learned value function and check it against what you would expect: states near the goal should be worth more, and if they are not, something is wrong that the reward curve did not tell you.
  *Checkpoints:* The update rule written out before the code → Implemented on a small environment and converging → Exploration schedule chosen and its effect shown → Learned value function inspected against intuition
- **M2.4 · Break it with function approximation** — assignment, 165 min, tier 2
  Replace your table with a neural network and watch it become unstable. Record what that looks like — diverging values, oscillation, catastrophic forgetting of an earlier good policy. Then remove one leg of the deadly triad: use on-policy data, or drop bootstrapping, or add a target network to slow the moving target. Measure the difference. The claim to defend is which ingredient you removed and why that specific one addressed what you actually saw.
  *Checkpoints:* The tabular version replaced with a function approximator → Instability produced and its signature recorded → One of the three destabilising ingredients removed and re-measured → The deadly triad named in terms of what was observed
- **M2.5 · The taxonomy of algorithms** — reading, 105 min, tier 0  
[Welcome to Spinning Up in Deep RL!](https://spinningup.openai.com/en/latest/)
  *Where:* Part 2, Kinds of RL Algorithms — the model-free split between policy optimisation and Q-learning, and what each trades away.
- **M2.6 · Retention: value methods and the deadly triad** — retention, 15 min, tier 1

### M3 — Policy gradients

*840 minutes*

- **M3.1 · Intro to policy optimisation** — reading, 180 min, tier 0  
[Welcome to Spinning Up in Deep RL!](https://spinningup.openai.com/en/latest/)
  *Where:* Part 3, Intro to Policy Optimization — the derivation of the simplest policy gradient, line by line, including why the log appears.
- **M3.2 · The policy gradient theorem** — reading, 150 min, tier 0  
[Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction](https://openlibrary.org/isbn/9780262039246)
  *Where:* Chapter 13 — the policy gradient theorem, REINFORCE, REINFORCE with baseline, and actor-critic.
- **M3.3 · REINFORCE, from the derivation** — assignment, 180 min, tier 1
  Derive the REINFORCE estimator in your own writing, then implement it. Train on a small control task. Run at least five seeds and plot all of them, not the mean alone — reinforcement learning curves are famously seed-sensitive, and a single run is a screenshot rather than a result. Report the spread. If two seeds solve the task and three never learn, that is the finding and it is entirely normal.
  *Checkpoints:* The gradient estimator derived in the submission → Implemented and learning on a small environment → At least five seeds run → Spread across seeds reported alongside the mean
- **M3.4 · Variance reduction, measured** — assignment, 165 min, tier 2
  Measure the variance of your gradient estimates directly rather than inferring it from how wobbly the reward curve looks. Add a baseline — a learned value function, or even a running mean of returns — and measure the variance again. Compare sample efficiency at equal environment steps rather than equal wall-clock. Then answer the question that makes the baseline legitimate: why does subtracting it not bias the estimator.
  *Checkpoints:* Gradient variance measured directly, not inferred from the reward curve → A baseline added and variance re-measured → Sample efficiency compared at equal environment steps → The bias question addressed
- **M3.5 · Reference implementations, afterwards** — reading, 150 min, tier 0  
[openai/spinningup](https://github.com/openai/spinningup)
  *Where:* The vpg and ppo implementations — read only after your own REINFORCE works, and read for the engineering details the derivations omit.
- **M3.6 · Retention: policy gradients and variance** — retention, 15 min, tier 1

*780 minutes*

- **M4.1 · InstructGPT, as a reinforcement learning paper** — reading, 150 min, tier 0  
[Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155)
- **M4.2 · The PPO trainer, against the DPO one** — reading, 120 min, tier 0  
[huggingface/trl](https://github.com/huggingface/trl)
  *Where:* The PPO trainer, read directly against the DPO trainer you used in AIE-205 — the interesting content is everything PPO needs that DPO does not.
- **M4.3 · Where the reward actually lands** — assignment, 180 min, tier 2
  Map RLHF onto the MDP vocabulary you learned in M1: what is the state, what is the action, when does the reward arrive. Then state the credit assignment problem precisely — a single scalar at the end of a sequence has to explain every token choice. Identify what the KL penalty is doing in reinforcement learning terms rather than as a regularisation trick. Finish by predicting one behavioural consequence and checking it against what the papers report.
  *Checkpoints:* The RLHF setup mapped onto the MDP vocabulary from M1 → The credit assignment problem stated concretely for token generation → The KL penalty's role identified in RL terms → One consequence for behaviour predicted and checked against the literature
- **M4.4 · Why DPO gets to skip all this** — assignment, 165 min, tier 2
  You ran DPO in AIE-205 as a method. Now explain what it is doing: it reparameterises the RLHF objective so the reward model disappears and the optimisation becomes supervised. Identify the assumption that makes this legal. Then construct a case where that assumption is questionable, and take a position — with reasons — on when the full PPO pipeline is still worth its considerable extra cost. This is the item that retroactively justifies putting this course in the programme.
  *Checkpoints:* The DPO objective related to the RLHF objective it approximates → The assumption that permits the reparameterisation identified → A case constructed where that assumption is questionable → A position taken on when PPO-style RLHF is still worth its cost

[Welcome to Spinning Up in Deep RL!](https://spinningup.openai.com/en/latest/)
  *Where:* The PPO page — the objective, the clipping, and the pseudocode.
- **M4.6 · Retention: RLHF and its relation to DPO** — retention, 15 min, tier 1

### M5 — Publish, and defend

*720 minutes*

- **M5.1 · rlhf-study, published** — project, 180 min, tier 3
  Publish the study the reinforcement-learning capability points at: your policy-gradient implementation with its honest seed spread, the reward-hacking failure you produced, and your position on when preference optimisation is enough. It must connect backwards to AIE-205, because the whole argument of this course is that the method you used there is an approximation to something, and this is where you say to what.
  *Checkpoints:* The policy-gradient implementation published with its seed spread → The written account of reward hacking included → The DPO-versus-PPO argument stated as a position, not a summary → Connected backwards to AIE-205
- **M5.2 · Make it hack the reward** — assignment, 165 min, tier 2
  Write a reward function that looks reasonable and contains an exploitable flaw, then train an agent until it finds the exploit. Describe what it did and show the reward it obtained. Then repair the reward and try again. The second round is the point: reward specifications are usually broken in more than one way, and the experience of fixing one and immediately finding another is the thing that transfers to writing evaluation criteria for anything, including the rubrics in this programme.
  *Checkpoints:* A reward function written with a plausible flaw → An agent trained that exploits it → The exploit described and its reward accounted for → The reward repaired and the new exploit found, or its absence argued
