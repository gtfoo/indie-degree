# AIE-300 — Capstone

**6 credits · 120 hours · 6 modules**

> One substantial system that exercises the whole programme, shipped publicly and defended aloud. This is the artifact the transcript is built around, and it is the only course whose output a hiring manager will actually open. Everything before it was practice with a rubric attached; this is the thing itself, with the same rubrics applied to work nobody set for you.

One substantial system that exercises the whole programme, shipped publicly and defended aloud. This is the artifact the transcript is built around.

**By the end you can**

- Ship a system that a stranger can use, with published evaluation results
- Report honestly on what did not work and what the numbers actually say
- Defend the design under unscripted questioning
- Write it up so a hiring manager can assess it in ten minutes

**Sitting rule.** Every item is completable in one sitting. Nothing exceeds 180 minutes, and anything over 90 declares its own checkpoints. A 120-hour project is 45 sittings, and the granularity is deliberate — it is what makes an irregular schedule survivable.

| Assignment | 100.8 | 84% |
| Project | 14.8 | 12% |

## The panel

- **Judges** `claude-opus-5`, `gpt-5`, `gemini-flash-latest`
- **Blind** Judges see the submission and the rubric, never each other's scores, and never the learner's self-assessment.
- **Disagreement** Scores are reported per judge and never averaged into a single number when the spread is 2 or more on any criterion.

## Modules (46 items)

### M1 — Scope, and commit to it in writing

*1080 minutes*

- **M1.1 · Three candidates, and the criteria that choose between them** — assignment, 180 min, tier 1
  Write the criteria for choosing a capstone before you write the candidates: who it is for, what evidence it can produce, what it would cost to run, and whether it can be evaluated at all. Then describe three genuinely different systems and score each. Record the runner-up's strongest argument, because in four weeks you will wonder whether you picked wrong and the honest answer should already be written down.
  *Checkpoints:* Selection criteria written before the candidates → Three genuinely different systems described → Each scored against the criteria → The winner chosen and the runner-up's strongest argument recorded
- **M1.2 · Does this need AI at all** — assignment, 180 min, tier 2
  Apply AIE-107 to your own capstone before building it. Isolate the core task and specify a non-AI approach in enough detail that you could cost it — rules, a lookup, a classical model, or a human doing it. Cost both at a realistic volume. If the honest answer is that the system does not need a model, change the capstone rather than the answer; a capstone chosen because it needs AI is a better capstone than one that merely tolerates it.
  *Checkpoints:* The core task isolated → A non-AI approach specified in enough detail to cost → Both approaches costed at a stated volume → The decision recorded with the conditions that would reverse it
- **M1.3 · The evaluation plan, registered before any code** — assignment, 180 min, tier 1
  Write and register the evaluation before writing any code: what number defines success, what threshold you will accept, what the negatives look like, and which baseline the system must beat. Commit it with a timestamp. Every course in this programme has argued that the measurement must predate the thing measured, and this is the item where you do it for work of your own choosing.
  *Checkpoints:* The success criterion stated as a number with a threshold → The eval method specified, including negatives → A baseline named that the system must beat → Committed to a timestamped record before implementation begins
- **M1.4 · What you will not build** — assignment, 180 min, tier 2
  List at least six things a reasonable person would expect this system to do, and exclude them. Give the reason for each. Then allocate the remaining 120 hours across the phases below and name the trigger that will make you cut further — a date, or an hours-spent threshold. Capstones fail by scope, not by difficulty, and a scope you have not written down is one you will quietly expand.
  *Checkpoints:* At least six excluded features listed → Each with the reason for exclusion → The 120-hour budget allocated across the remaining modules → A named trigger for cutting further
- **M1.5 · Data handling and risk, before there is data** — assignment, 165 min, tier 1
  List every category of data this system will touch, including logs and traces. Decide retention and deletion for each before the first row exists, because retrofitting deletion is how personal data ends up living forever in a backup. Then name the failure that would most embarrass you in public and either mitigate it or write down that you are accepting the risk and why.
  *Checkpoints:* Every category of data the system will touch listed → Retention and deletion decided per category → The failure that would most embarrass you identified → A mitigation or an accepted-risk statement for each
- **M1.6 · Publish the scope document** — project, 180 min, tier 3
  Publish the scope document publicly before you write code. It carries the selection, the exclusions, the registered evaluation and the baseline, and it states plainly what existed before this capstone started so the boundary of new work is checkable by a reader. Publishing it in advance is what makes every later claim about intent verifiable rather than reconstructed.
  *Checkpoints:* Scope, exclusions, evaluation plan and baseline in one public document → What already existed before the capstone stated explicitly → Published before implementation begins → Linked from the public log
- **M1.7 · Retention: the registered scope and its thresholds** — retention, 15 min, tier 1

### M2 — Build

*1800 minutes*

- **M2.1 · The walking skeleton** — assignment, 180 min, tier 1
  Build the thinnest possible end-to-end path and deploy it: request in, trivial answer out, logged. No intelligence yet. Doing this first means every later component lands in a system that already exists rather than accumulating toward one, and it moves the deployment problem to the day when nothing is at stake.
  *Checkpoints:* End-to-end path working with the simplest possible behaviour → Deployed somewhere reachable from the internet → One real request served and logged → Committed and reproducible from a clean checkout
- **M2.2 · The core capability, first pass** — assignment, 180 min, tier 1
  Make the system do its actual job, badly, on real inputs. Record why you chose the model and the approach. Start a file of failure cases the moment you see the first one — these become the regression corpus later, and the ones you notice now are the ones you will have forgotten by then.
  *Checkpoints:* The main task working on a handful of real inputs → Prompt or model choice recorded with its reason → Known failure cases collected as they appear → No evaluation yet — that is M3
- **M2.3 · Context and retrieval, or the argument for not having it** — assignment, 180 min, tier 1
  Decide what the model needs to see and implement it — retrieval, a fixed context, or nothing. Justify the choice against the properties of your actual corpus, as AIE-103 argued: dense proper nouns favour lexical search, near-synonymous prose favours embeddings. If you decline retrieval entirely, that is a legitimate answer and it needs the same justification. State the context budget in tokens, because you now know what those cost.
  *Checkpoints:* A context strategy chosen and implemented, or explicitly declined → The choice justified against the corpus's actual properties → Context budget stated in tokens → Retrieval evaluated separately from generation, if present
- **M2.4 · Output that cannot be malformed** — assignment, 180 min, tier 1
  Define a schema for every model output the system relies on and validate at the boundary. When validation fails, retain the invalid payload — a rejected response that is discarded is a bug you cannot investigate. Prove the path works by injecting a malformed response deliberately, because this code runs rarely and is therefore the code most likely to be wrong.
  *Checkpoints:* A schema defined for every model output the system depends on → Validation at the boundary, with the invalid payload retained → Behaviour on validation failure defined and tested → A deliberate malformed response injected to prove the path
- **M2.5 · Failing closed** — assignment, 180 min, tier 1
  Enumerate what happens when each external dependency fails: the model API, the datastore, the network. Set every timeout explicitly — an inherited default is a decision someone else made about your product. Define what the user sees when the system is degraded, and make it honest rather than a spinner. Then simulate a real failure and confirm the system does what you said.
  *Checkpoints:* Every external dependency's failure mode enumerated → Timeouts set explicitly rather than inherited → The degraded behaviour defined and visible to the user → A dependency failure simulated end to end
- **M2.6 · Cost and latency instrumented from the start** — assignment, 180 min, tier 1
  Record tokens, cost and per-stage latency for every request from the first real one. AIE-105 argued that cost is a design constraint rather than a bill you read later, and instrumenting on day one is the difference between knowing what a change cost and estimating it. Compute a cost per thousand requests now, while the number is small enough to be surprising.
  *Checkpoints:* Per-request token counts and cost recorded → Latency recorded per stage, not only end to end → A cost-per-thousand-requests figure computed → The data written somewhere queryable
- **M2.7 · An interface a stranger can use** — assignment, 180 min, tier 1
  Build the interface to the standard of someone arriving with no context. State the system's limits in the interface rather than in a README nobody opens. Write error messages for a person who does not know your architecture. Then watch one real person use it without helping them, and write down every point at which you wanted to intervene.
  *Checkpoints:* The primary task completable without instruction → The system's limits stated in the interface itself → Error states written for someone who does not know the internals → Tested by watching someone use it without help
- **M2.8 · Deploy it properly** — assignment, 180 min, tier 1
  Make the deployment reproducible and boring. Secrets outside the repo, state outside the artifact, and a rollback you have actually performed rather than one you believe exists. This programme runs on a box where a failed build once overwrote a running server's assets and the service reported healthy while serving nothing — deployment failures are quiet, and the only defence is having tested the recovery.
  *Checkpoints:* Reproducible deployment from a clean checkout → Secrets outside the repository and outside the tree → State outside the deployable artifact → A rollback path tested, not assumed
- **M2.9 · Observability, so failures are visible** — assignment, 180 min, tier 1
  Make it possible to answer 'what went wrong for that user' without reading every log line. Retain full traces for a window you state, consistent with the data decisions you already registered. Add one check that would catch the system being entirely broken, because the failure that costs most is the one nobody notices for a week.
  *Checkpoints:* Full traces retained for a stated window → A way to find the requests that went wrong without reading everything → An alert or check that would catch total failure → Retention consistent with the M1.5 data decisions
- **M2.10 · The first external user** — assignment, 165 min, tier 2
  Put the system in front of at least one person who is not you and does not work on it, with their consent to observe. Do not help them. Log every point of confusion and every assumption they made that you did not expect. Make one change in response and check whether it helped. This is the item that separates a capstone from a demo, and it is routinely skipped because it is uncomfortable.
  *Checkpoints:* At least one person who is not you uses it unaided → Their session observed or recorded with consent → Every point of confusion logged → One change made in response, and its effect checked
- **M2.11 · Retention: the system's architecture and failure paths** — retention, 15 min, tier 1

### M3 — Evaluate, against the plan you registered

*1440 minutes*

- **M3.1 · Build the eval set the plan specified** — assignment, 180 min, tier 1
  Build the evaluation set your M1.3 plan specified, at the size it specified, with the negatives it specified. Record where each case came from. Load it into tools/eval-harness so the format is validated rather than trusted — the harness rejects a corpus with untagged cases or spans that are not verbatim in their source, and those are exactly the errors that make a gold set quietly wrong.
  *Checkpoints:* Cases built to the registered specification → Negatives included at the stated proportion → Every case carrying its provenance → Loaded and validated by the harness
- **M3.2 · Run it, and record the baseline** — assignment, 180 min, tier 1
  Score the system through the harness and read the per-tag results rather than the headline number. Freeze the run as a baseline. Then compare against the threshold you registered in M1.3 and state plainly whether you hit it. This is the moment the registered plan pays for itself, and it is the moment most projects renegotiate their success criterion instead.
  *Checkpoints:* The system scored through the harness → Per-tag results reported, not only the aggregate → A baseline frozen for future comparison → The result compared against the registered threshold
- **M3.3 · Error analysis on real traces** — assignment, 180 min, tier 2
  Read at least fifty real traces one at a time and code the failures as you find them rather than sorting them into categories you decided in advance. Count and rank the categories. Then take the largest and find its actual cause in the system. AIE-102 argued that error analysis beats eval design as a first move; this is where you do it on a system you built to your own specification.
  *Checkpoints:* At least 50 real traces read individually → Failures open-coded rather than bucketed in advance → Categories counted and ranked → The top category traced to a specific cause
- **M3.4 · Beat the baseline you named** — assignment, 180 min, tier 2
  Implement the baseline you named in the registered plan, and implement it in good faith. Evaluate both on the identical set. State the comparison with an interval rather than two numbers. If the baseline wins, that is the finding, it goes in the write-up, and it is worth more to a reader than a system that beat nothing.
  *Checkpoints:* The M1.3 baseline implemented properly, not as a straw man → Both evaluated on the identical set through the same harness → The comparison stated with an interval → A verdict recorded even where it is unflattering
- **M3.5 · How much of this is noise** — assignment, 180 min, tier 2
  Run the same evaluation several times without changing anything and measure how much the number moves. Compute an interval. Then state the smallest difference this eval could detect — that figure governs every improvement claim you will make for the rest of the project, and knowing it stops you reporting a two-point gain that your own harness cannot distinguish from nothing.
  *Checkpoints:* The eval re-run several times with sampling enabled → Run-to-run variation quantified → An interval computed for the headline number → The smallest detectable difference stated
- **M3.6 · A regression suite that runs itself** — assignment, 180 min, tier 2
  Turn the failure file you have been keeping since M2.2 into a regression suite, wire it to run automatically, and make it fail the build on regression against the frozen baseline. Then break something on purpose and confirm it fires. A check that has never been seen to fail is a check nobody should trust, and this programme has already caught two of those in its own tooling.
  *Checkpoints:* Every failure case from M2.2 onward turned into a case → The suite wired to run automatically → Non-zero exit on regression against the frozen baseline → One deliberate regression introduced to prove it fires
- **M3.7 · Act on the error analysis, and measure** — assignment, 180 min, tier 2
  Make one change targeting the largest failure category, and predict its effect before making it. Re-evaluate. Compare the outcome against your prediction and against the smallest detectable difference from M3.5. A gain smaller than your noise floor is not a gain, and reporting it as one is the most common dishonesty in applied machine learning.
  *Checkpoints:* One change made addressing the top failure category → Expected effect predicted before the change → Re-evaluated on the identical set → Result compared against both the prediction and the noise floor
- **M3.8 · Where this system stops working** — assignment, 165 min, tier 2
  Find where your system stops working and write it down with numbers: input length, domain distance, language, ambiguity, volume. Every capability claim in this programme requires a documented negative result, and this is the item that produces one for the capstone. Anyone can ship a demo; the boundary is the part a reader learns from.
  *Checkpoints:* The failure boundary probed deliberately → The conditions under which quality collapses identified → The boundary quantified rather than described → Recorded as the capability's negative result
- **M3.9 · Retention: the system's measured behaviour and limits** — retention, 15 min, tier 1

### M4 — Harden

*1080 minutes*

- **M4.1 · Red-team your own system** — assignment, 180 min, tier 2
  Attack your own system deliberately. Enumerate every path by which text you did not write reaches the model — user input, retrieved documents, tool results, file names. Attempt injection through each. Record what worked, exactly. Apply mitigations and try again. The attacks that still work after mitigation go in the write-up, because a security section listing only solved problems tells a reader nothing.
  *Checkpoints:* An attack surface enumerated for this specific system → Prompt injection attempted through every input path → Successful attacks recorded verbatim → Mitigations applied and re-tested
- **M4.2 · The controls that actually hold** — assignment, 180 min, tier 1
  Implement the controls the system actually needs — access control if there is anything to protect, rate limiting with a threshold you chose rather than inherited, bounded input sizes. Test each by attempting to violate it. An unbounded input field in front of a per-token bill is a funding request from a stranger.
  *Checkpoints:* Authentication and authorisation implemented if there is anything to protect → Rate limiting in place with a stated threshold → Input size bounded → Each control tested by attempting to violate it
- **M4.3 · What it costs under load** — assignment, 180 min, tier 2
  Define a realistic load, run the system under it, and compare cost and latency against your single-request numbers from M2.6. They will not scale linearly. Find the point at which the system stops meeting its latency target or its cost stops being acceptable, and state it as a number of concurrent users. A system whose breaking point is unknown is one you cannot responsibly show anyone.
  *Checkpoints:* A load profile defined and justified → The system run under it → Cost and latency at load compared against the single-request figures → The breaking point identified
- **M4.4 · The failure drill** — assignment, 180 min, tier 1
  Break the system on purpose in three ways and recover from each: the model provider unavailable, the datastore unavailable, and a bad deploy. Record how long detection took and how long recovery took. The numbers matter less than discovering which failure you had no plan for, which is always one of them.
  *Checkpoints:* The model provider's failure simulated → The datastore's failure simulated → A bad deploy performed and rolled back → Time to detect and time to recover recorded for each
- **M4.5 · What you would not ship to a paying customer** — assignment, 165 min, tier 2
  Write the list you would give a colleague inheriting this system: what is not production-grade, what risk each thing carries, and roughly what it would cost to fix. Then say which you would actually do and which you would accept. This list is the single most credible thing in a capstone write-up, because everyone who has shipped anything knows it exists and most authors pretend theirs does not.
  *Checkpoints:* An honest list of what is not production-grade → Each item's risk stated concretely → The work required to close each estimated → A judgement on which would be worth doing
- **M4.6 · The runbook someone else could operate from** — assignment, 180 min, tier 1
  Write the document that lets someone who is not you keep this running: how to deploy, how to roll back, where the secrets and the data and the logs live, and what to do for each failure you drilled in M4.4. Then test it the only way that works — follow it exactly, without using anything you know that is not written down. Every step you had to improvise is a step that was missing, and finding them now is cheaper than finding them during an outage.
  *Checkpoints:* Every routine operation documented as steps, not prose → Each failure from the M4.4 drill given a procedure → Where the secrets, the data and the logs live, stated → Tested by following it exactly, without improvising
- **M4.7 · Retention: attack surface and operational limits** — retention, 15 min, tier 1

### M5 — Write it up, and ship it

*1080 minutes*

- **M5.1 · The ten-minute assessment** — project, 180 min, tier 3
  Write the document a hiring manager reads in ten minutes and then decides. What it does and for whom in the first paragraph; the headline number with its interval near the top; the limitations where they cannot be missed; a link that works. Most capstone write-ups bury the result under a narrative of the journey. Yours puts the result first and the journey underneath, where the reader can choose it.
  *Checkpoints:* What it does, for whom, in the first paragraph → The headline number with its interval, above the fold → The limitations visible without scrolling past praise → A working link a reader can use immediately
- **M5.2 · The technical write-up** — project, 180 min, tier 3
  Write the version for someone who wants to argue with you. Every significant decision, its alternative, and whether it was measured or inherited — the distinction AIE-204 introduced, and the one almost no published write-up makes. Describe the evaluation in enough detail that a reader could attack it, because an evaluation nobody can criticise is one nobody can trust.
  *Checkpoints:* Every significant decision recorded with its alternative → Each marked as measured or inherited → The evaluation method described well enough to criticise → The error analysis and its consequences included
- **M5.3 · The chapter that connects backwards** — project, 180 min, tier 3
  Publish the capstone chapter into the growing public log. It must stand alone and must connect to at least three earlier chapters — and it must revise at least one earlier conclusion, because after twelve courses and a shipped system, something you wrote in the first six weeks is now wrong. Finding it and correcting it in public is the point of keeping one log rather than six posts.
  *Checkpoints:* Published into the public engineering log → Connected to at least three earlier chapters → At least one earlier conclusion revised → Stands alone on its own page
- **M5.4 · The limitations section, written honestly** — assignment, 180 min, tier 2
  Write the limitations from the measurements rather than from modesty. The failure boundary from M3.8, every unfavourable number, and the honest gap between what M1.6 registered and what you actually delivered. Then check your draft against your published scope document and confirm nothing quietly disappeared between them.
  *Checkpoints:* The negative result from M3.8 stated prominently → Every unfavourable measurement included → The gap between registered scope and delivered system stated → Nothing removed between draft and publication
- **M5.5 · Ship it publicly** — project, 165 min, tier 3
  Put it in the world. A public URL that works without you, source public unless you can state why not, and a running cost you can sustain. Say how long you intend to keep it up — a capstone that quietly disappears in three months makes every link in the transcript dead, and stating a horizon in advance is more honest than letting it rot.
  *Checkpoints:* Live at a public URL a stranger can reach → Source public, or the reason for keeping it closed stated → Running cost stated and sustainable → A stated intention for how long it will stay up
- **M5.6 · Update the capability claims this earns** — assignment, 180 min, tier 2
  Go through the capability pages and decide what this system actually earns. For each claim you make, name the specific evidence. For each one it does not support, say so and leave it unearned. The temptation at the end of a capstone is to let one system justify every line on the CV, and refusing that is the whole reason the evidence ledger exists.
  *Checkpoints:* Each capability this system evidences identified → The specific evidence named per claim → Claims the system does not support explicitly declined → The artifact and negative-result fields updated where earned
- **M5.7 · Retention: the write-up's claims and their evidence** — retention, 15 min, tier 1

### M6 — Defend

*720 minutes*

- **M6.1 · The questions you are afraid of** — assignment, 180 min, tier 2
  Write the fifteen questions you least want to be asked about this system, aimed at real weaknesses rather than easy ones. Draft answers. Identify the three you cannot answer well, and put those into the write-up as acknowledged limitations rather than hoping nobody asks. A question you have already conceded in writing cannot be used against you.
  *Checkpoints:* At least fifteen questions written, targeting real weaknesses → Answers drafted for each → The three with no good answer identified → Those three addressed in the write-up rather than hidden
- **M6.2 · The capstone defense** — defense, 180 min, tier 4
  The longest defense in the programme, because it covers a whole system rather than one assignment. Questions are generated at defense time from your published write-up and repository. Answered aloud, without notes and without the system open in front of you. At least one line of questioning is pursued past the point of comfort, because the difference between a system you built and one you assembled shows up three questions deep, not one.
  *Checkpoints:* Questions generated at defense time from the published artifacts → Answered aloud without notes and without the system open → At least one hostile line of questioning pursued to its end → Recording retained
- **M6.3 · Correct what the defense exposed** — assignment, 180 min, tier 2
  The defense will expose errors — a number you could not justify, a decision you could not reconstruct, a claim broader than the evidence. Correct the published write-up rather than leaving it and adding a note. Date the correction and leave it visible. A public document that quietly changes is worse than one that was wrong, and a visible correction is the strongest possible evidence that the rest is honest.
  *Checkpoints:* Every point where the defense revealed an error listed → The write-up corrected rather than annotated → The correction dated and visible → Anything that turned out to be unfixable stated as such
- **M6.4 · What you would do differently** — assignment, 165 min, tier 2
  Name the largest thing you got wrong, and the moment at which it was still cheap to catch. Then convert that into a practice rather than a resolution — 'register the evaluation before building' is a practice, 'be more careful' is not. Adopt one of them formally, by writing it into the rules of a repository you own, so the lesson survives your memory of this project.
  *Checkpoints:* The largest wrong decision identified → The point at which it could have been caught named → What would have caught it stated as a practice, not a resolution → One practice actually adopted
- **M6.5 · Retention: the capstone, cold** — retention, 15 min, tier 1
