# AIE-205 — Fine-tuning and Post-training

**3 credits · 60 hours · 5 modules**

> Where a base model becomes a product, and where the evaluation discipline from AIE-102 stops being optional. The single question this course exists to answer honestly is the one most fine-tuning write-ups avoid: did it beat a well-prompted baseline on the same eval set? Frequently it does not, and a course that cannot produce that answer has taught a technique rather than a judgement.

Where a base model becomes a product, and where evaluation discipline from AIE-102 stops being optional.

**By the end you can**

- Curate a fine-tuning dataset and defend every inclusion rule
- Run LoRA fine-tuning and measure whether it actually helped
- Explain preference optimisation and implement DPO
- Detect and report regression on capabilities you did not intend to change

**Sitting rule.** Every item is completable in one sitting. Nothing exceeds 180 minutes, and anything over 90 declares its own checkpoints.

## The panel

- **Judges** `claude-opus-5`, `gpt-5`, `gemini-flash-latest`
- **Blind** Judges see the submission and the rubric, never each other's scores, and never the learner's self-assessment.
- **Disagreement** Scores are reported per judge and never averaged into a single number when the spread is 2 or more on any criterion.

## Modules (30 items)

### M1 — The dataset is the model

*780 minutes*

- **M1.1 · The post-training pipeline, end to end** — lecture, 180 min, tier 0  
[Deep Dive into LLMs like ChatGPT](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7xTGNNLPyMI)
- **M1.2 · Training language models to follow instructions** — reading, 150 min, tier 0  
[Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155)
- **M1.3 · Inclusion rules, written before the data** — assignment, 180 min, tier 1
  Write the inclusion and exclusion rules for a fine-tuning dataset before you look at any candidate data, then collect at least a hundred candidates and judge every one against the rules. Count the rejections by rule. You will find at least one rule that rejects nothing, which means it was decoration, and at least one that turns out to be ambiguous in practice. Revise once and log what changed. The dataset is the model, and rules invented while filtering are rules fitted to the data you happened to have.
  *Checkpoints:* Inclusion and exclusion rules written before any example is collected → At least 100 candidate examples assembled → Every example accepted or rejected against the written rules → Rejections counted by rule, and the rules revised once with the revision logged
- **M1.4 · How ambiguous are your own rules** — assignment, 150 min, tier 2
  Take fifty examples you already judged, wait at least a day, and judge them again without looking at your earlier answers. Measure how often you agree with yourself. Anything below near-total agreement is a measurement of how ambiguous your rules are, not of your concentration — and it puts a ceiling on what any model trained on this data can learn. Trace each disagreement to a rule and rewrite the rules that caused them.
  *Checkpoints:* A sample re-judged after a gap of at least a day → Self-agreement measured on identical items → Disagreements traced to specific rules → Rules rewritten where the disagreement was linguistic rather than substantive
- **M1.5 · Dataset engineering, and when not to fine-tune** — reading, 105 min, tier 0  
[AI Engineering](https://openlibrary.org/isbn/9781098166304)
  *Where:* The chapters on dataset engineering and on the decision of whether to fine-tune at all — read the second one first.
- **M1.6 · Retention: data curation and its ceiling** — retention, 15 min, tier 1

### M2 — Supervised fine-tuning, and the baseline that must beat it

*720 minutes*

- **M2.1 · Fine-tuning, implemented** — reading, 180 min, tier 0  
[Build a Large Language Model (From Scratch)](https://openlibrary.org/isbn/9781633437166)
  *Where:* The chapters on fine-tuning for classification and on instruction fine-tuning — including the loss masking, which is the detail most implementations get wrong silently.
- **M2.2 · Llama 2, for the post-training section** — reading, 120 min, tier 0  
[Llama 2: Open Foundation and Fine-Tuned Chat Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.09288)
- **M2.3 · Run a supervised fine-tune** — assignment, 180 min, tier 1
  Fine-tune a small open model on the dataset you curated. Mask the loss so that only the completion tokens contribute — verify this by checking that the prompt tokens' gradients are zero, because getting it wrong produces a model that trains without error and learns to predict its own prompts. Evaluate through the harness on the gold set that already existed, not on a metric invented afterwards.
  *Checkpoints:* A small open model chosen and its size stated → Loss masked so only completion tokens contribute → Training and validation loss recorded → Evaluated on the AIE-102 gold set through the harness
- **M2.4 · The prompted baseline that has to lose first** — assignment, 150 min, tier 2
  Spend real effort prompting the base model to do the same task — few-shot examples drawn from the same data your fine-tune saw, a time-box you state in advance, and the iterations logged. Evaluate on the identical gold set through the same harness. Then state the comparison with an interval rather than two bare numbers. If the prompted baseline wins, that is the finding, and it is the most commercially useful sentence in this course.
  *Checkpoints:* A genuine prompt-engineering effort on the base model, time-boxed and logged → Few-shot examples drawn from the same training data → Evaluated on the identical gold set → The comparison stated with an interval, not a point
- **M2.5 · Post-training in a from-scratch course** — lecture, 75 min, tier 0  
[Stanford CS336 Language Modeling from Scratch I 2025](https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLoROMvodv4rOY23Y0BoGoBGgQ1zmU_MT_)
  *Where:* The post-training and alignment lectures — cite the course playlist, since the individually reuploaded copies are not authorised.
- **M2.6 · Retention: SFT mechanics and loss masking** — retention, 15 min, tier 1

### M3 — Parameter-efficient methods

*720 minutes*

- **M3.1 · LoRA** — reading, 120 min, tier 0  
[LoRA: Low-Rank Adaptation of Large Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.09685)
- **M3.2 · The configuration that actually matters** — reading, 120 min, tier 0  
[huggingface/peft](https://github.com/huggingface/peft)
  *Where:* The conceptual guides on LoRA — rank, alpha, and which modules to target, which is the choice with the largest effect and the least discussion.
- **M3.3 · Rank is a dial with a cost** — assignment, 180 min, tier 1
  Train the same fine-tune at three LoRA ranks. Report trainable parameters, gold-set score, wall-clock and peak memory for each. Plot score against trainable parameters. The interesting region is where the curve flattens, because that is the rank above which you are paying for nothing — and it is usually lower than the defaults suggest.
  *Checkpoints:* Three ranks trained on identical data and schedule → Trainable parameter count reported per rank → Gold-set score reported per rank → Training time and memory recorded per rank
- **M3.4 · Where the low-rank assumption runs out** — assignment, 165 min, tier 2
  LoRA constrains the update to a low-rank subspace, which is an assumption about how much the weights need to move. Construct or choose a task where you expect that assumption to bind — a large domain shift rather than a format change — and predict the outcome before running it. Compare against full fine-tuning if compute allows; if it does not, say so and substitute a smaller model where it does. State the assumption in mechanical terms: what kind of change can a rank-r update not express.
  *Checkpoints:* A task chosen where LoRA is expected to struggle, with the reason stated first → LoRA and full fine-tuning both run where compute allows → The gap measured, or the substitute experiment justified → The low-rank assumption stated in terms of what it constrains
- **M3.5 · What the Trainer does on your behalf** — reading, 120 min, tier 0  
[huggingface/transformers](https://github.com/huggingface/transformers)
  *Where:* The Trainer documentation and one example fine-tuning script — read specifically for the defaults it applies without asking, since those are the settings you will be blamed for.
- **M3.6 · Retention: LoRA mechanics and rank selection** — retention, 15 min, tier 1

### M4 — Preference optimisation

*720 minutes*

- **M4.1 · Direct preference optimisation** — reading, 150 min, tier 0  
[Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)
- **M4.2 · The DPO trainer, against the paper** — reading, 120 min, tier 0  
[huggingface/trl](https://github.com/huggingface/trl)
  *Where:* The DPO trainer documentation and its example, read alongside the paper's objective so the implementation's extra terms are visible as choices.
- **M4.3 · The DPO loss, from the equation** — assignment, 180 min, tier 1
  Implement the DPO loss from the equation in the paper, not from the library. Handle the reference model explicitly — the frozen policy's log-probabilities are where implementations diverge quietly. Verify against the library on identical inputs and weights. Then show what beta does by running two values and describing the behaviour, since it controls how far the policy is allowed to move and is the parameter people set by copying.
  *Checkpoints:* The loss implemented directly from the paper's equation → Reference-model log-probabilities handled explicitly → Verified against the library implementation on identical inputs → The role of beta demonstrated at two values
- **M4.4 · Run DPO, and find what else moved** — assignment, 165 min, tier 2
  Build a small preference dataset — pairs where you can state why one response is preferred — and run DPO from your SFT checkpoint. Measure the behaviour you targeted. Then measure at least two things you did not target and did not want to change. Preference optimisation moves the whole policy, and the untargeted measurements are the point of the assignment.
  *Checkpoints:* A small preference set built with the preference criterion stated → DPO run from an SFT checkpoint → The targeted behaviour measured before and after → At least two untargeted capabilities measured before and after
- **M4.5 · Constitutional AI** — reading, 90 min, tier 0  
[Constitutional AI: Harmlessness from AI Feedback](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.08073)
- **M4.6 · Retention: preference optimisation** — retention, 15 min, tier 1

### M5 — Did it help, and what did it cost

*660 minutes*

- **M5.1 · Regression sweep on what you did not intend to change** — assignment, 180 min, tier 2
  Define a set of capabilities you are not trying to change — instruction following on unrelated tasks, formatting, refusal behaviour, another language — and score the base model on all of them before fine-tuning. Score the fine-tuned model on the identical set afterwards. Report each individually. Averaging hides exactly the failure this exists to find, which is a large regression in one capability concealed by small gains in several.
  *Checkpoints:* A capability set defined before fine-tuning, covering behaviours not being targeted → Base model scored on all of them → Fine-tuned model scored on the identical set → Regressions reported individually rather than as an average
- **M5.2 · fine-tune-study, published** — project, 180 min, tier 3
  Publish the study that the fine-tuning capability claim points at: the curation rules, what the data looked like, the training configuration, and the comparison against a well-prompted baseline on a gold set that predated the model. The comparison goes near the top, not in a discussion section at the end. It must connect backwards to AIE-102's gold set and to the harness that ran it, because the fact that the measurement existed first is the strongest thing about this piece of work.
  *Checkpoints:* Public write-up with the dataset rules, the training configuration and the eval → The prompted baseline comparison stated prominently rather than buried → A negative result included → Connected backwards to the AIE-102 gold set and the harness
- **M5.3 · The alignment tax, measured on your own model** — assignment, 120 min, tier 2
  Pick a safety behaviour the base model exhibits and measure it before your SFT, after your SFT, and after DPO. Fine-tuning on narrow task data routinely erodes behaviours nobody intended to touch, and reporting a loosening in your own model is the honest outcome here. Suggest a mechanism for what you observe, and mark it as a suggestion rather than a finding.
  *Checkpoints:* A refusal or safety behaviour identified in the base model → Measured before and after both SFT and DPO stages → Any change reported including a loosening → The mechanism suggested rather than asserted
- **M5.4 · Oral defense** — defense, 60 min, tier 4
  Three unscripted questions generated from your own submissions at defense time, answered aloud without notes. This defense carries the fine-tuning capability claim.
  *Question shapes:*
    - Your self-agreement on the labels was below ceiling. What does that put a limit on, and how would you have raised it?
    - You chose rank 16. Talk me through what a rank-4 update cannot express that a rank-16 one can, without using the word 'capacity'.
    - Your prompted baseline beat the fine-tune. A stakeholder has already told the client you are fine-tuning. What do you say to them?
- **M5.5 · The whole pipeline, briefly, from the outside** — lecture, 105 min, tier 0  
[Intro to Large Language Models](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zjkBMFhNj_g)
- **M5.6 · Retention: the post-training pipeline and its costs** — retention, 15 min, tier 1
