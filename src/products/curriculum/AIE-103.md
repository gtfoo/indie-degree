# AIE-103 — Retrieval and Context Systems

**3 credits · 50 hours · 9 modules**

> Retrieval is the default answer to most enterprise AI questions and is wrong often enough that knowing when not to use it is the differentiator. This course refuses the word RAG for as long as possible, because it welds together two separate problems — finding the right thing, and writing a good answer from it — and most broken systems are broken in only one of them.

Retrieval is the default answer to most enterprise AI questions and is wrong about a third of the time. Knowing when not to use it is the differentiator.

**By the end you can**

- Build hybrid retrieval and show where lexical beats dense, with numbers
- Choose a chunking strategy from measured retrieval quality rather than from a blog post
- Evaluate retrieval separately from generation, and locate which half is failing
- Articulate when long context replaces retrieval and when it does not
- Enforce document-level access control through a retrieval pipeline, and prove it holds

**Sitting rule.** Every item is completable in one sitting. Nothing exceeds 180 minutes, and anything over 90 declares its own checkpoints.

### M1 — Two problems wearing one name

*345 minutes*

[Beyond the Basics of Retrieval Augmented Generation](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0nA5QG3087g) — *Hamel Husain*

- **M1.2 · CMU Advanced NLP (10): Retrieval and RAG** — lecture, 90 min, tier 0  
[CMU Advanced NLP Fall 2024 (10): Retrieval and RAG](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KfQaYk4k9eM) — *Graham Neubig*
  The same territory derived properly — sparse and dense retrieval from first principles, then long-context transformers as the competing answer.
- **M1.3 · Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks** — reading, 45 min, tier 0  
[Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)
- **M1.4 · Bi-encoders and the embedding model landscape** — reading, 45 min, tier 0  
[Pretrained Models — Sentence Transformers](https://sbert.net/docs/sentence_transformer/pretrained_models.html)
- **M1.5 · Separate the two problems** — assignment, 75 min, tier 2
  Take a question your own product could plausibly answer from documents. Write down, before building anything: what 'the right thing was retrieved' would mean, how you would check it without looking at the generated answer, and what a good answer from bad retrieval would look like. That last one is the trap the rest of the course exists to avoid.

### M2 — Embeddings and the limits of similarity

*360 minutes*

- **M2.1 · Dense Passage Retrieval** — reading, 45 min, tier 0  
[Dense Passage Retrieval for Open-Domain Question Answering](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.04906)
- **M2.2 · BEIR: zero-shot evaluation of retrieval models** — reading, 60 min, tier 0  
[BEIR: A Heterogenous Benchmark for Zero-shot Evaluation of Information Retrieval Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08663)
  The paper that showed dense retrievers losing to BM25 out of domain. Read it before assuming embeddings win, because your instinct will be that they do.
- **M2.3 · A dense baseline over both corpora** — assignment, 150 min, tier 1
  Build embedding retrieval over the Carpark SG rate documents and over Indie Degree's resource corpus. Write 25 real queries per corpus with the correct document labelled first — real questions you would actually ask, not questions written to be easy. Report recall@5 and MRR for both. This is the number everything later is compared against.
  *Checkpoints:* both corpora indexed → 25 labelled queries each → recall@5 and MRR reported
- **M2.4 · Find where embeddings fail** — assignment, 90 min, tier 1
  Construct ten queries your dense baseline gets wrong, and classify why. At least four must fail because the query contains an identifier, code or acronym that the embedding blurs into its neighbours. Show the retrieved results, not just the failure count.
- **M2.5 · Retention: embedding retrieval and its failure modes** — retention, 15 min, tier 1

### M3 — Chunking, measured

*375 minutes*

- **M3.1 · Introducing Contextual Retrieval** — reading, 60 min, tier 0  
[Introducing Contextual Retrieval](https://www.anthropic.com/news/contextual-retrieval)
  Chunking destroys the context a chunk needed in order to be findable. This is the fix, and it comes with numbers you are going to try to reproduce.
- **M3.2 · Searching for Best Practices in RAG** — reading, 45 min, tier 0  
[Searching for Best Practices in Retrieval-Augmented Generation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.01219)
- **M3.3 · Four chunking strategies, one metric** — assignment, 150 min, tier 1
  Compare fixed-size, fixed-size with overlap, structural (by heading or record), and one strategy of your own design. Same embedding model, same query set, same everything else. Report recall@5 per strategy per corpus. Then answer the question that matters: is the winner the same on both corpora, and if not, what property of the documents decides it?
  *Checkpoints:* four strategies implemented → held constant except chunking → per-corpus results and a stated deciding property
- **M3.4 · Contextual retrieval, replicated** — assignment, 105 min, tier 1
  Implement Anthropic's contextual retrieval on your winning chunking strategy: prepend model-generated context to each chunk before embedding. Report the improvement, the token cost of generating it, and whether you would pay that at your corpus size. A replication that fails to reproduce the reported gain is a full-credit outcome if the experiment is sound.
  *Checkpoints:* contextualised chunks generated → improvement measured → cost per corpus refresh stated
- **M3.5 · Retention: chunking strategy and its measurement** — retention, 15 min, tier 1

### M4 — Hybrid and lexical search

*375 minutes*

- **M4.1 · Reciprocal Rank Fusion** — reading, 45 min, tier 0  
[Reciprocal Rank Fusion outperforms Condorcet and individual Rank Learning Methods](https://plg.uwaterloo.ca/~gvcormac/cormacksigir09-rrf.pdf)
  Short, old, and still the method people reach for. Note how little it assumes.
- **M4.2 · ColBERT: late interaction** — reading, 60 min, tier 0  
[ColBERT: Efficient and Effective Passage Search via Contextualized Late Interaction over BERT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.12832)
- **M4.3 · Make BM25 beat your embeddings** — assignment, 150 min, tier 1
  Implement BM25 over both corpora and run the same query sets. The claim to test is specific: on Carpark SG's rate documents, lexical search should beat your dense baseline, because the queries carry codes and official terminology. Report both, and if BM25 does not win, work out why before concluding the claim is false.
  *Checkpoints:* BM25 implemented on both corpora → same query sets run → result reported with the winner explained
- **M4.4 · Fuse them** — assignment, 105 min, tier 1
  Combine dense and lexical with reciprocal rank fusion. Report whether the fusion beats the better of its two inputs on each corpus — it frequently does not, and reporting that honestly is worth more than a tuned weighting that beats it on 50 queries.
  *Checkpoints:* fusion implemented → compared against the better single method → verdict stated per corpus
- **M4.5 · Retention: lexical scoring and fusion** — retention, 15 min, tier 1

### M5 — Reranking

*300 minutes*

- **M5.1 · Cross-encoders and rerankers** — reading, 45 min, tier 0  
[Pretrained Models — Cross Encoder](https://sbert.net/docs/cross_encoder/pretrained_models.html)
  The cross-encoder section specifically. Note why it cannot be used for first-stage retrieval, and why that is the whole design.
- **M5.2 · HyDE: zero-shot dense retrieval without relevance labels** — reading, 45 min, tier 0  
[Precise Zero-Shot Dense Retrieval without Relevance Labels](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.10496)
- **M5.3 · Rerank, and pay for it** — assignment, 120 min, tier 1
  Add a cross-encoder reranker over your best first-stage retriever. Report the quality gain, the added latency per query at p50 and p95, and the cost. Then state the candidate-set size at which the gain stops being worth the latency, with the measurement that shows it.
  *Checkpoints:* reranker integrated → quality and latency both measured → break-even candidate-set size stated
- **M5.4 · When reranking loses** — assignment, 75 min, tier 2
  Find queries the reranker makes worse — where the correct document was in the first-stage results and reranking demoted it. Explain the pattern. Every reranker has these; a submission that finds none has not looked.
- **M5.5 · Retention: two-stage retrieval and its costs** — retention, 15 min, tier 1

### M6 — Evaluating retrieval separately from generation

*330 minutes*

- **M6.1 · RAGAS: automated evaluation of retrieval augmented generation** — reading, 45 min, tier 0  
[RAGAS: Automated Evaluation of Retrieval Augmented Generation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.15217)
- **M6.2 · RAGAS in practice** — reading, 60 min, tier 0  
[explodinggradients/ragas](https://github.com/explodinggradients/ragas)
  *Where:* The metrics module — faithfulness, answer relevancy, context precision and context recall.
- **M6.3 · Retrieval metrics that mean something** — assignment, 120 min, tier 1
  Build a retrieval eval that runs unattended over both corpora and reports recall@k, MRR and nDCG.   *Checkpoints:* harness runs unattended → three metrics computed → dashboard metric chosen and justified
- **M6.4 · Locate the failing half** — assignment, 90 min, tier 1
  Take ten cases where the final generated answer is wrong. For each, determine whether retrieval failed or generation failed, and state the evidence that decides it. Report the split. If you cannot decide for a case, that is a finding about your instrumentation.
- **M6.5 · Retention: retrieval metrics and attribution** — retention, 15 min, tier 1

### M7 — When long context replaces retrieval

*255 minutes*

- **M7.1 · Lost in the Middle** — reading, 45 min, tier 0 · **optional**  
[Lost in the Middle: How Language Models Use Long Contexts](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172)
  *Optional because:* Lost in the Middle is required reading in AIE-101 M5.2. Re-read here only if the long-context argument in M7.3 does not land without it.
- **M7.2 · Retrieval Augmented Generation or Long-Context LLMs?** — reading, 60 min, tier 0  
[Retrieval Augmented Generation or Long-Context LLMs? A Comprehensive Study and Hybrid Approach](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.16833)
- **M7.3 · The crossover point** — assignment, 150 min, tier 1
  For one of your corpora, compare your best retrieval pipeline against simply putting the whole corpus in the context window, on the same queries. Report answer quality, cost per query and latency for both. Then state the corpus size at which your answer flips, and show the arithmetic. This is the question a customer will actually ask you, and 'it depends' is not an answer they can act on.
  *Checkpoints:* both approaches run on the same queries → quality, cost and latency for each → crossover size derived with arithmetic shown

### M8 — Production retrieval

*330 minutes*

- **M8.1 · Sensitive information disclosure** — reading, 45 min, tier 0  
[OWASP Top 10 for Large Language Model Applications](https://owasp.org/www-project-top-10-for-large-language-model-applications/)
  *Where:* The sensitive-information-disclosure entry, and the excessive-agency entry alongside it.
- **M8.2 · Retrieval that respects permissions** — assignment, 150 min, tier 1
  Every enterprise retrieval conversation reaches this question and most demos have no answer. Give your index documents with owners and visibility, then enforce that a query only ever retrieves what its caller may see - enforced at retrieval, not by filtering the answer afterwards, because a model that has read a document it should not have has already leaked it. Then prove it: query as a user who should see nothing, and show that nothing comes back.
  *Checkpoints:* documents carry ownership and visibility → filtering happens before the model sees anything → negative case demonstrated
- **M8.3 · Ingestion and staleness** — assignment, 120 min, tier 1
  Documents change. Build the ingestion path - add, update, delete - and measure how long a change takes to become visible to a query. Then answer the question a customer asks next: what does the system do when it retrieves a document that has since been superseded, and how would anyone notice?
  *Checkpoints:* add, update and delete paths working → propagation delay measured → stale-retrieval behaviour stated
- **M8.4 · Retention: access control, ingestion and freshness** — retention, 15 min, tier 1

### M9 — Course project

*390 minutes*

- **M9.1 · Ship retrieval into a product** — project, 150 min, tier 3
  Put retrieval into one of your live products, publicly reachable. The design must follow from your own measurements rather than from defaults — if you ship dense-only, the report has to show why hybrid lost on that corpus.
  *Checkpoints:* retrieval integrated → design traced to a measurement → deployed and reachable
- **M9.2 · The retrieval report** — project, 105 min, tier 2
  Write up the whole sequence: baseline, chunking, lexical, fusion, reranking, and the long-context comparison. Every technique that did not help must appear with its number. A report in which everything worked is a report that was not measured.
  *Checkpoints:* all techniques reported including failures → numbers traceable to the harness → recommendation stated
- **M9.3 · Publish** — project, 75 min, tier 3
  Publish this as the next chapter of your public engineering log — one growing site, not a sixth standalone post. The chapter must stand alone on its own page for a reader arriving cold, and must connect backwards to at least one result from an earlier chapter. Publish at a stable public URL with the eval harness reachable, so a reader can rerun it against their own corpus.
- **M9.4 · Oral defense** — defense, 60 min, tier 4
  Three unscripted questions generated from your report at defense time, answered aloud without notes.
  *Question shapes:*
    - A customer has 200 documents and wants an answer bot. From your numbers, do they need retrieval at all?
    - Your reranker added latency. Describe the user for whom that trade was wrong.
    - Which of your results do you least trust, and what would you run to check it?
