## Modules (31 items)

### M1 — Autodiff, written from scratch

*900 minutes*

- **M1.1 · What backpropagation is actually computing** — lecture, 120 min, tier 0  
[Neural networks](https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLZHQObOWTQDNU6R1_67000Dx_ZCJB-3pi)
  *Where:* Chapters 3 and 4 — backpropagation, and the calculus behind it. Chapters 1 and 2 were AIE-201 M5.1; this is the other half.
- **M1.2 · Building micrograd** — lecture, 180 min, tier 0  
[Neural Networks: Zero to Hero](https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLAqhIrjkxbuWI23v9cThsA9GvCAUhRvKZ)
  *Where:* The spelled-out introduction to neural networks and backpropagation — the video that builds a scalar autodiff engine from nothing.
- **M1.3 · Your own autodiff engine** — assignment, 180 min, tier 1
  Write a scalar reverse-mode autodiff engine: a value type that records the operations performed on it, and a backward pass that walks the graph in topological order accumulating gradients. Do not consult micrograd's source while writing it; consult it afterwards and note the differences. Verify against torch.autograd on an expression with at least one repeated variable, because that is where naive implementations silently overwrite instead of accumulating.
  *Checkpoints:* Forward pass with a graph that records its own operations → Backward pass implemented with topological ordering → Gradients matched against torch.autograd on the same expression → One non-trivial function — a division or a power — added and checked
- **M1.4 · Train something with the engine you wrote** — assignment, 180 min, tier 1
  Build a small multi-layer perceptron out of your own engine and train it on something real enough to have a wrong answer — a two-class problem from your own data, or a standard small dataset. Get the loss down. Then train the same architecture in PyTorch and compare both the final loss and the wall-clock time. The gap in speed is the point at which you understand what a framework is actually buying you.
  *Checkpoints:* An MLP assembled from your own value type → A training loop with a stated loss and optimiser → Loss decreasing on a real task → The same task trained in PyTorch for comparison
- **M1.5 · How autograd works in the framework** — reading, 120 min, tier 0  
[Welcome to PyTorch Tutorials](https://pytorch.org/tutorials/)
  *Where:* The tensors tutorial and the autograd mechanics tutorial — the dynamic graph, leaf tensors, and when gradients are freed.
- **M1.6 · Where your engine and the framework part company** — assignment, 105 min, tier 1
  Name at least three things PyTorch does that your engine does not — graph freeing, in-place operation tracking, broadcasting, vectorisation, higher-order gradients. For each, say what it buys and what it costs, and demonstrate one of them with a snippet where your engine would give the wrong answer or run out of memory. This is a comparison, not an apology: a scalar engine is not trying to be a framework.
  *Checkpoints:* At least three concrete differences identified → Each stated as a design trade rather than a defect → One demonstrated with code
- **M1.7 · Retention: reverse-mode autodiff** — retention, 15 min, tier 1

### M2 — Training dynamics, and reading a run

*840 minutes*

- **M2.1 · The internals of a training run** — lecture, 180 min, tier 0  
[Neural Networks: Zero to Hero](https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLAqhIrjkxbuWI23v9cThsA9GvCAUhRvKZ)
  *Where:* The videos on manual backpropagation through a two-layer network, and on the internals — activation and gradient statistics, and what their histograms look like when training is healthy.
- **M2.2 · Why optimisation in deep networks is its own problem** — reading, 150 min, tier 0  
[Deep Learning](https://www.deeplearningbook.org/)
  *Where:* Chapter 8, sections 8.2 on ill-conditioning, plateaus and cliffs, and 8.7 on optimisation strategies. AIE-201 read this chapter for the algorithms; this is the same chapter for the pathologies.
- **M2.3 · Instrument a training run** — assignment, 180 min, tier 1
  Take a network that trains successfully and instrument it: activation means and standard deviations per layer, gradient norms per layer, and the ratio of update magnitude to weight magnitude. Record all of it across training. Then write down what healthy looks like as numbers — the ranges you observed — because a diagnostic you can only apply by squinting at a plot is one you will not apply at 2am.
  *Checkpoints:* Activation statistics recorded per layer per epoch → Gradient norms recorded per layer → Update-to-weight ratio computed → A healthy run characterised numerically, not visually
- **M2.4 · Three broken runs, and the tell for each** — assignment, 180 min, tier 2
  Break the same training run three ways — a dead network from bad initialisation, a diverging one from too high a learning rate, and one that trains but does not generalise. Apply your instrumentation to each and find the earliest point at which they are distinguishable from one another and from the healthy run. Then have someone, or a script, hand you the four runs unlabelled and see whether your procedure identifies them. Report your accuracy at that, including if it is poor.
  *Checkpoints:* Three distinct failures produced from one base run → The instrumentation from M2.3 applied to each → The earliest distinguishing signal identified for each → A diagnostic procedure written and then tested blind
- **M2.5 · A second pass over the same ground** — lecture, 135 min, tier 0  
[MIT Introduction to Deep Learning \| 6.S191](https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLtBw6njQRU-rwp5__7C0oIVt26ZgjG9NI)
  *Where:* The introduction to deep learning and deep sequence modelling lectures — a different lecturer covering the same material, which is the cheapest way to find out whether you understood it or merely followed it.
- **M2.6 · Retention: training pathologies and their signatures** — retention, 15 min, tier 1

### M3 — Initialisation, normalisation, regularisation

*780 minutes*

- **M3.1 · Training neural networks, the practical lectures** — lecture, 150 min, tier 0  
[CS231n Winter 2016](https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLkt2uSq6rBVctENoVBg1TpCC7OQi31AlC)
  *Where:* The training-neural-networks lectures — activation functions, weight initialisation, batch normalisation, and babysitting the learning process.
- **M3.2 · Regularisation, properly** — reading, 150 min, tier 0  
[Deep Learning](https://www.deeplearningbook.org/)
  *Where:* Chapter 7, Regularization for Deep Learning — through 7.12 on dropout, including 7.8 on early stopping as a regulariser.
- **M3.3 · Initialisation decides whether it learns** — assignment, 180 min, tier 1
  Initialise the same deep network three ways — all zeros, a naive Gaussian with fixed scale, and a fan-in-aware scheme such as He or Xavier. Before training, measure the activation variance at every layer and show it growing or collapsing with depth. Then train all three and report what happened. Finish by connecting the measurement to the derivation: the scheme exists to keep that variance constant, and your numbers either show that or they do not.
  *Checkpoints:* Three initialisation schemes implemented → Activation variance measured layer by layer at step zero → Training outcome recorded for each → The measured variance related to the scheme's derivation
- **M3.4 · Does normalisation help here, or is it cargo** — assignment, 165 min, tier 2
  Add batch or layer normalisation to a network you have trained, and find out whether it actually helps on your problem. Tune the learning rate separately for each version, because normalisation's main effect is often on how tolerant the network is of the learning rate rather than on final accuracy — and comparing at a single shared rate hides that entirely. Run several seeds. Report both effects separately, and be willing to conclude it made no difference.
  *Checkpoints:* A baseline without normalisation, tuned → Normalisation added and re-tuned rather than dropped in → Both compared across multiple seeds → The effect on learning rate sensitivity measured separately from final accuracy
- **M3.5 · Numerical stability, and the standard regularisers** — reading, 120 min, tier 0  
[Dive into Deep Learning](https://d2l.ai/)
  *Where:* The chapters on numerical stability and initialisation, and on weight decay and dropout — read for the runnable code alongside the equations.
- **M3.6 · Retention: initialisation and normalisation** — retention, 15 min, tier 1

### M4 — Architectures, and reading a diagram into code

*720 minutes*

- **M4.1 · Understanding LSTM networks** — reading, 120 min, tier 0  
[Understanding LSTM Networks](https://colah.github.io/posts/2015-08-Understanding-LSTMs/)
- **M4.2 · Convolutional networks and their case studies** — lecture, 150 min, tier 0  
[CS231n Winter 2016](https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLkt2uSq6rBVctENoVBg1TpCC7OQi31AlC)
  *Where:* The convolutional network lectures and the architecture case studies — AlexNet through ResNet, and what each was solving.
- **M4.3 · Implement an architecture from its diagram alone** — assignment, 180 min, tier 1
  Pick an architecture with a published diagram — a small ResNet block, an LSTM cell, or an encoder block — and implement it from the diagram and the paper's text alone. Do not open a reference implementation until you have finished. Compute the parameter count by hand from the shapes and check it against what the framework reports; a mismatch means you misread the diagram, and finding out where is the assignment. Then open the reference and list what you got wrong.
  *Checkpoints:* Architecture chosen and its diagram cited → Implemented without consulting a reference implementation → Parameter count computed by hand and checked against the model → Reference implementation consulted afterwards and differences listed
- **M4.4 · Convolutional and recurrent architectures, with code** — reading, 135 min, tier 0  
[Dive into Deep Learning](https://d2l.ai/)
  *Where:* The chapters on convolutional neural networks and on recurrent neural networks — the implementations alongside the explanations.
- **M4.5 · Ablate the component nobody questions** — assignment, 120 min, tier 2
  Take the architecture you implemented and remove one component that is normally taken for granted — the skip connection, the gate, the normalisation layer. Keep the parameter count roughly constant so you are testing the component and not the capacity. Run several seeds and report whether it earned its place on your problem at your scale. The honest answer at small scale is frequently that it did not, and saying so is worth more than reproducing the expected result.
  *Checkpoints:* One component removed cleanly → Parameter count held roughly constant, or the difference accounted for → Multiple seeds run → A conclusion stated about whether it earns its place
- **M4.6 · Retention: architectural components and what each fixes** — retention, 15 min, tier 1

### M5 — Scale, and a network of your own

*660 minutes*

- **M5.1 · Scaling laws for neural language models** — reading, 120 min, tier 0  
[Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.08361)
- **M5.2 · Training compute-optimal models** — reading, 105 min, tier 0  
[Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.15556)
  *Where:* Read against the previous paper: the interesting content is where the two disagree about how to spend a fixed compute budget, and why.
- **M5.3 · A scaling curve you measured yourself** — assignment, 180 min, tier 2
  Train the same architecture at four or more sizes on a laptop, to convergence, and plot final loss against parameter count or against compute. You will get a curve. Then do the part that separates this from a plot: say what it does and does not license. Four small points over one order of magnitude cannot support an extrapolation to a model a thousand times larger, and the papers you just read are the standard of evidence you are being compared against.
  *Checkpoints:* At least four model sizes trained to convergence → Compute accounted for per run → Loss plotted against a compute or parameter axis → The limits of extrapolating from this range stated
- **M5.4 · The network you built, and what its curves showed** — project, 180 min, tier 3
  Publish a chapter of the public engineering log covering what you built and, more importantly, what the instrumentation showed. Include at least one thing that did not work — the ablation that came out flat, the normalisation that made no difference, the scaling curve that was too short to conclude from. It must connect backwards to an earlier chapter; the autodiff engine and the AIE-201 backpropagation derivation are the obvious links.
  *Checkpoints:* Written for a reader who has trained nothing → Instrumentation plots included with their ranges explained → At least one negative result included → Published and connected backwards to an earlier chapter
- **M5.5 · Oral defense** — defense, 60 min, tier 4
  Three unscripted questions generated from your own submissions at defense time, answered aloud without notes.
  *Question shapes:*
    - Your engine accumulated gradients for a repeated variable. Walk me through what would have happened if it had assigned instead, and at which point in training you would have noticed.
    - You concluded batch norm made no difference. A reviewer says you must have tuned it wrong. Answer them.
    - Your scaling curve has four points. Someone extrapolates it two orders of magnitude in a meeting. What do you say?
- **M5.6 · Retention: scale, compute and what a curve supports** — retention, 15 min, tier 1
